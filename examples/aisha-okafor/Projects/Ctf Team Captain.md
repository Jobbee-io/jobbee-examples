# Captain of Kudzu Overflow, a CTF team

Kudzu Overflow, an amateur capture-the-flag team, 2020 to now. I co-founded it and have been captain since January 2022. On my resume it's one line under "Outside work". This file covers what the captain does, our results, and the three times I got it wrong.

## What it is

Four of us on Lanternwatch's night shift started the team in 2020 to play online CTFs on our days off. In jeopardy-style events you solve standalone challenges for points, in categories like web, crypto, forensics and reverse engineering. In attack-defense events every team runs the same vulnerable services and has to patch its own while exploiting everyone else's. We've had 14 members over time and have 9 active: SOC analysts, two software engineers, a network engineer and a college student. My categories are web and forensics.

## What the captain does

Mostly not solving. I pick events, set the roster, assign people to categories at the start, and decide when someone should rotate off a challenge. Between events I run a practice night every other week. On a good weekend I score 20–30% of our points, but our results depend more on triage than on my solves.

During an event:

- Before it starts, I post the challenge list by category with starting assignments. People swap if they want; I fill the gaps.
- Every 4 hours, a 10-minute voice check-in: who's stuck, who's close, who should go to sleep. Most of our late points come from these.
- After 90 minutes stuck, you post what you've tried, then hand off or pair up.
- Within a day of the end, every solve has a named writer and a named reviewer who didn't solve it. The writeup is due within a week.

## How it went

- 2020–2021: casual, about 8 events a year, results in the middle of the pack.
- January 2022: I became captain and brought in three rules: rotate after 90 minutes stuck, a reviewed writeup for every solve, and nobody alone on a challenge for a whole event.
- 2022 and 2023: 11 events, then 12. Results improved, and so did the fatigue.
- 2024 to now: capped at 6 events a year, chosen together in January.

## Results

- Best online result: 11th of about 900 teams in a 48-hour online jeopardy CTF run by a university security club (2024). Typical result: top 10%.
- First in-person event: an invite-only attack-defense final in Atlanta, October 2024. 6th of 16 teams.
- 52 public writeups at github.com/example/kudzu-overflow, about 40% of them web challenges.

## What went wrong

- The 2024 attack-defense final. I spent about 5 hours on one web exploit because I was sure I was close. Nobody owned patching our own services, and one of them was exploited for 3 straight rounds before anyone noticed. The lost service points probably cost us 2 places. I'd broken my own rotation rule and left the most important job unassigned. Now, in attack-defense, defense is assigned first, one person owns it full-time, and the captain doesn't take on a long exploit.
- Burnout in 2023. Twelve events, mostly weekends, on top of jobs that still included night shifts for some of us. Three members quit within two months, two of them people I'd recruited. I had measured the season by placements and never asked how people were doing. The cap of 6 events and a no-guilt opt-out per event came from the conversation I should have started much earlier. I ask about load every quarter now.
- A newcomer in 2022 spent a whole event alone on a challenge nobody helped with, and didn't come back. New members now pair with a regular for their first two events.

Nobody on this team reports to me or is paid to be there. Triage, deciding who works on what, getting people to write things down, and noticing when the pace is wrong are the same skills I use running the review program at work.
