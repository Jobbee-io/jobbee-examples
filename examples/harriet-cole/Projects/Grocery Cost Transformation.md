# Grocery cost programme: the store labour workstream

Corriemoor Partners, for a UK grocery retailer. March 2022 – February 2023, as a Consultant. On my resume this is one bullet with 4 numbers: £31m identified, £28m approved, £23m implemented, £21m realised. This overview covers the programme, my part in it, the timeline, the decisions and who disagreed. Companion files: `Grocery Cost Transformation Store Labour Model.md` (how the model works, the pilot it failed, and the rebuild), `Grocery Cost Transformation Savings Tracker.md` (how each stage was counted, lever by lever).

## What it was, and why

The client runs about 420 stores, about 170 large supermarkets and about 250 convenience stores, with about £5.8bn of revenue. Food inflation and wage rises in 2022 were squeezing margin, and the CFO had set a target of £90m of cost out over 3 years before we arrived. Corriemoor ran a 12-week diagnostic (March – May 2022), then supported implementation for 9 months (June 2022 – February 2023).

The diagnostic identified about £104m across 5 workstreams: store labour, goods-not-for-resale procurement, distribution, online fulfilment and head office. Store labour was the biggest cost line in scope, about £610m a year or roughly 10.5% of sales, and the biggest workstream at £31m. Store hours had been set for years as last year's hours adjusted for sales growth. Nobody could say how many hours a store should need for the work it actually did.

## My role

The team was a partner, an engagement manager and 4 to 6 consultants. I was one of the consultants, and from week 3 of the diagnostic I owned the store labour workstream, reporting to the engagement manager.

Mine: the labour model, the 14 store visits, the pilot measurement plan, and the weekly savings tracker I ran with the client's finance business partner. Not mine: the engagement manager owned the storyline for the executive team, other consultants ran the other 4 workstreams (one of them owned online picking), the client's store operations planning team changed the rotas, and HR ran the consultation with the recognised union. Store managers made the changes in stores, not us. I don't claim any number from the other workstreams.

## Timeline

- March 2022: diagnostic starts. Store labour is mine from week 3.
- March – April: I build the task-based labour model on 2 years of till, delivery and rota data for all 420 stores, and do 14 store visits, including 2 night shifts on replenishment. I'd worked nights part-time at university, and the night shifts showed me what the head office data didn't: late delivery arrivals wasted more hours than any task.
- May: £31m identified, by lever.
- June: the executive team approves £28m. Vacancies in stores above the model stop being backfilled. I set up the tracker with the finance business partner, on scheduled hours.
- August: the pilot in 8 older stores. The tracker moves to paid hours and the reported savings fall £1.4m in one week.
- Early September: we stop the pilot cut in week 4.
- September – October: I rebuild the time standards in 3 layout bands. HR runs a 6-week consultation with the union. Every store manager gets a 2-week window to challenge their hours.
- November: wave 1 goes live in 110 stores. A capex freeze moves the self-checkout expansion to the next year. Stores freeze changes for Christmas trading from mid-November.
- January – February 2023: waves 2 and 3 go live in the other 310 stores. At the end of February, £23m is implemented, and the model and tracker go to the client's planning and finance teams.
- April 2024: the client's year-end programme report shows £21m realised in FY2023/24.

## The levers, and the ladder

Savings are run-rate at constant volume, against the FY2021/22 baseline of hours and hourly cost. Realised means paid hours from payroll, signed off by the client's finance team. The lever table and every definition are in the savings tracker file.

The levers were: hours set by the model instead of by history (£14m), night-fill replenishment in large stores (£8m), checkout staffing that followed the spread of self-checkouts (£3m), self-checkout expansion in 60 stores (£3m), and shorter opening hours in 40 convenience stores (£3m).

- Identified, May 2022: £31m.
- Approved, June 2022: £28m. The executive team dropped the opening-hours lever, for customer reasons. I think that was the right call.
- Implemented, February 2023: £23m. The £3m self-checkout expansion moved to the next year after the capex freeze, and £2m went back into hours after the pilot.
- Realised, FY2023/24: £21m, about 3.4% of store labour cost. About £1.4m was lost to overtime in roughly 30 stores, and about £0.6m to 7 stores where local management reversed night-fill.

About two-thirds of the hours came out through vacancies not backfilled, and the rest through contracted-hours changes agreed with the union. There was no redundancy programme in stores.

## Decisions, and who disagreed

- Net, not gross. The model put 291 stores above their modelled hours and 87 below. The programme office wanted the £17.1m of hours above the model as the opportunity. I netted off the £3.1m of hours the 87 stores needed and reported £14m. Store managers had to trust the model for 9 months of rollout, and nobody trusts a model that only ever cuts. The engagement manager backed me and the CFO agreed.
- Night-fill only where deliveries allowed it. Because of the night shifts, I made night-fill depend on delivery times as well as layout: only large stores whose back room could hold a night's delivery and where at least 85% of deliveries had arrived before 22:00 over 13 weeks. 96 of about 170 qualified. The store operations director wanted all large stores. We agreed the 96 would go first and the rest would wait until the distribution workstream fixed arrival times.
- How progress was reported. The programme's board pack called rota changes "delivered". In October 2022 I asked for implemented and realised as separate columns, and the finance director agreed. That's where the 4-stage ladder on my resume comes from.

## What went wrong

The pilot. I took the time standards from 12 high-performing stores, and all 12 had been refitted in the last 5 years. The 8 pilot stores were older, with smaller back rooms and narrower aisles. We cut their replenishment hours by 14%. Within 4 weeks, on-shelf availability fell from 96.1% to 94.3% and complaints about empty shelves roughly doubled. Store managers had told me on my visits that older layouts were slower. I'd written it down and not acted on it. We stopped the cut, I rebuilt the standards in 3 layout bands, and availability was back to 96.0% by week 10. The rebuild was worth £2m less, and that's the honest price of my shortcut. Detail in the model file.

The tracker. For the first 2 months I tracked scheduled hours from the rotas. When I switched to paid hours, overtime in about 30 stores cut the reported savings by £1.4m in one week. It was the right change and an awkward meeting with the client's finance director. I should have started with paid hours. Detail in the tracker file.

## After we left

- The client's store operations planning team runs the model. By the April 2024 report they had rebuilt it inside their workforce scheduling system.
- The overtime I found in August 2022 was still there in FY2023/24, in about the same 30 stores. Area managers got a weekly overtime report, but overtime needed no approval.
- 74 large stores were still on daytime replenishment at my handover, waiting on delivery times.
- I know the realised number because the client's year-end report came to Corriemoor in April 2024 with a request for follow-on work, and I checked it with my former counterpart, the finance business partner. I have nothing after FY2023/24.

## What I'd do differently

- Take time standards from a sample that matches the estate, not from the best stores.
- Track paid hours from day 1, agreed with finance before the first report.
- Give store managers the challenge window before the pilot, not after it.
- Make overtime approval part of the lever, not a report that follows it.
