# The pricing engagement I got wrong

Corriemoor Partners, for a UK booking-software company. March – May 2025, plus an unbilled follow-up in February 2026. My first engagement as day-to-day lead. On my resume it's one bullet and the first entry under failures.

## What it was

The client sells booking and payments software to small businesses such as salons, clinics and studios: about £38m ARR and about 21,000 customers. Monthly logo churn (accounts cancelled in a month divided by accounts at the start of it) rose from 1.9% to 2.6% during 2024. The CEO's brief was to fix pricing and packaging.

## My role

I led the engagement day to day with 2 consultants. The partner sold it and joined weekly. I wrote the hypothesis and the storyline, chose the analyses, built the pricing model and presented the recommendation. The consultants ran the competitor scan and the customer survey, which a research agency fielded. The client's product and finance teams supplied the data and launched the change. The hypothesis was mine.

## The hypothesis, and the evidence I collected for it

In week 1 I wrote: small customers churn on price, because new low-cost competitors charge about £25 a month and the entry plan, Essentials, is £45. Everything I gathered pointed the same way:

- Small accounts (1 or 2 staff calendars, about 60% of accounts) churned at 3.4% a month in the second half of 2024; larger accounts at 1.6%.
- In the 2024 exit survey, 46% of 640 respondents chose "too expensive".
- A survey of 900 current customers showed price sensitivity in the small segment.
- 6 competitors had launched or cut entry prices in 18 months.

I recommended a new Starter plan at £29 with fewer features, Essentials unchanged, and £5 more a month on Pro. My model said small-account churn would fall from 3.4% to 2.6% and net ARR would rise about £1.3m in year 1. The client launched it on 1 September 2025, to all customers at once.

## How I found out

The engagement ended at the recommendation. I asked the partner whether I could go back after 6 months and measure it. The client agreed to share data, Corriemoor agreed not to bill, and I spent 4 days on it in February 2026.

- Small-account churn: 3.3% a month (October 2025 – January 2026), against 2.6% modelled.
- 17% of existing Essentials customers moved down to Starter in 5 months. I'd modelled 6%.
- Average revenue per account fell 4%. More new customers signed up on Starter, but not enough to make up for it. Net ARR effect: about −£0.9m annualised, against +£1.3m modelled.

## What was actually going on

The data I needed was in the client's data warehouse in March 2025. My data request asked for billing and CRM data, not product usage. With usage data the pattern was plain. New accounts that took fewer than 20 bookings through the system in their first 30 days churned at 4.1% a month over months 2 to 12. Accounts that took 20 or more churned at 1.2%. 38% of new accounts never reached 20, and 71% of the churned customers who chose "too expensive" had never reached 20 bookings.

"Too expensive" meant "not worth it to me", and it wasn't worth it because they'd never got the product working. The problem was activation. A cheaper plan gave existing customers a reason to pay less and did nothing for the ones who never got set up.

## What went wrong, most mine first

1. I wrote the answer in week 1 and spent 9 weeks collecting evidence for it. Nothing in my plan could have proved it wrong.
2. I treated a stated exit reason as the cause.
3. I answered the question the engagement was sold on, pricing, without testing it.
4. I didn't ask for usage data, and nobody on the team noticed it was missing.
5. I recommended a full launch when the client could have tested Starter on new sign-ups against a control group.

## What the client did next

In March 2026 they closed Starter to existing customers and offered it to new customers on annual billing only, which stopped the downgrades. In April they started an onboarding pilot with a setup call in the first 7 days. The COO told me in June 2026 that the early cohorts look better. I haven't seen the data, so I don't count it.

## What I changed

- A kill test for every hypothesis: written in week 1 next to the hypothesis, saying what result would prove it wrong, and run first.
- Every data request includes behaviour data (product usage, not just billing), and I check what's missing before week 2.
- Where I can, I recommend a launch with a control group instead of a full rollout.
- A post-implementation review goes into every statement of work I write. I put one into my next engagement (`Commercial Operating Model.md`) and ran it in June 2026.

I only found out because I asked to go back. That's the plainest reason I want to work inside a company.
