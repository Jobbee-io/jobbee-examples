# Germany market entry: the recommendation was no

Corriemoor Partners, for a UK business payments fintech. 9 September – 15 November 2024, 10 weeks, as a Senior Consultant. On my resume this is one bullet. This file holds how we got to "don't enter", what I built, the paid test that settled it, what the client did with the answer, and what I got wrong. It's my clearest example of changing a decision with evidence.

## What it was, and why

The client sells multi-currency business accounts and supplier payments to UK small and mid-sized businesses: about 38,000 customers and about £60m revenue, after a Series D round. The board plan said: enter Germany in mid-2025, invest £14m over 2 years (an EU licence, a local team of 25, marketing), and break even in 30 months. We were hired to answer how to enter, not whether.

## My role

The team was a partner (2 days a week), an engagement manager, me, another consultant and an analyst. I ran 2 of the 3 workstreams, market sizing and customer economics. I built the entry business case model and designed the paid test with the client's growth marketing team, who ran the ads. The other consultant covered competitors and pricing, and the analyst ran the survey with a research agency. The engagement manager owned the storyline and the weekly steering meeting, and the partner owned the CEO and the board. The client's General Counsel and external lawyers owned the licensing work.

## How it went

Weeks 1 and 2 went on a top-down market size, built from the cross-border payment flows of German SMEs. The board liked it. It couldn't answer the real question: could the company win customers at a cost that paid back?

From week 3 I worked bottom-up. A survey of 600 German SMEs, plus public business statistics, gave a serviceable market of about 68,000 firms: 10 to 249 employees, paying foreign suppliers at least monthly. The board plan had assumed 240,000, which was every SME with any foreign trade.

I didn't want to guess acquisition cost from UK numbers, so we ran a 5-week paid search and social test in German: a landing page, a waitlist, and 2 qualifying questions (company size, and how often they pay foreign suppliers). It cost £22,000.

By week 5 the economics said no. I told the engagement manager that day. We told the CFO informally in week 6 and the CEO in week 7, so the board didn't hear it first in a deck.

## The numbers, and their definitions

- Qualified sign-up: a waitlist sign-up from a company with 10 to 249 employees that pays foreign suppliers at least monthly. There were 41, all after I fixed the targeting. Cost per qualified sign-up was £390, counting the £16,000 spent after the fix (£537 counting all £22,000).
- CAC (customer acquisition cost): cost per qualified sign-up divided by the UK conversion rate from qualified sign-up to active customer, 38%. About £1,030 in Germany, against about £420 in the UK. I flagged 38% as optimistic for an unknown brand.
- Contribution per active customer per month: revenue minus payment and FX costs minus support cost. £31 in Germany (prices about 20% lower to match local providers, and smaller FX volumes) against £48 in the UK.
- Payback: CAC divided by monthly contribution. About 33 months in Germany, against a board threshold of 18. About 9 months for UK small businesses today.
- Break-even of the German business: 58 months in our base case (range 44 to 75), against 30 in the board plan.

The recommendation: don't enter Germany in 2025. Put part of the budget into UK businesses with 50 to 249 employees, where payback was about 10 months. Look at Germany again if any of 3 triggers is met: £210 or less per qualified sign-up in a repeat test (what an 18-month payback needs), an EU licence obtained for another reason, or a distribution deal with a German bank.

## What the client did

The board decided in December 2024 not to enter. It put £5m of the £14m into a UK mid-market sales push in 2025 and didn't commit the other £9m. In March 2026 the client re-ran the German test itself: £350 per qualified sign-up, still above the trigger, and they're still not in Germany. I know this from a call with the CFO in June 2026. I haven't seen their data.

## What went wrong

- The licence timeline. Since Brexit, a UK payments firm needs its own authorisation in an EU country to serve German customers. I put the client's internal estimate of 9 months into the model without asking the external lawyers. Their estimate arrived in week 7: 12 to 18 months. It made the answer more clearly no, but I'd treated regulation as a footnote when it was a core input.
- The paid test. For the first 10 days the keywords were too broad and brought in freelancers and sole traders, who could never qualify. About £6,000 went on traffic we couldn't use. On day 11 I added the size question to the landing page and excluded freelancer search terms. The qualifying questions should have been there on day 1.
- The first 2 weeks on a top-down market size. That work was for the board's comfort, not for the decision.

## What I'd do differently

Start with unit economics, not market size. Bring the regulatory owner in during week 1. Design every paid test around the qualification rule before spending any money.
