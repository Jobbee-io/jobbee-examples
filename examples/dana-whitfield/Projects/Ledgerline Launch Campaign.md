# The Ledgerline launch campaign: one resume bullet, nine months of work

Verity Cloud, January to September 2023. On my resume this is one bullet: "$8.2M influenced pipeline in two quarters, 340 design partners pre-GA, 71% of launch-page visitors reaching the docs." This file holds what's behind it: the plan, the people, the definitions behind each number, and what didn't work.

## What it was

Ledgerline was Verity's data-lakehouse product: an open table format with query engines on top, running in the customer's own cloud account. It was a tier-1 corporate launch. At Verity that means the CEO keynote slot, a press embargo, field enablement for about 400 sellers, and a budget large enough that finance asks for the measurement plan before approving it. Ours was $2.4M, not counting headcount.

I owned the narrative and the campaign plan, and I was accountable for the launch number. I was one of three PMMs on the product. I worked with:
- a demand-generation lead, who owned channels and budget execution;
- two developer advocates;
- the docs lead;
- the product's engineering director;
- a field-enablement manager.

I managed none of them. The plan worked because each of them signed the exit criteria before we started.

## Positioning, and the test that changed it

The product team's draft line was "the lakehouse for every cloud." Before anything shipped, I ran it through the messaging test program. I posted a draft landing page and a two-paragraph architecture summary under my own name, in a data-infrastructure leaders' group and a practitioner forum, and asked for the red pen.

It broke in a day. "Every cloud" meant three clouds at GA, and four commenters said so within hours. Worse, the main reason teams were evaluating lakehouses was missing from the copy: getting rid of nightly copy jobs between the warehouse and the lake.

The shipped line was "One copy of your data, three clouds, no nightly copy jobs." It's narrower. It's also the version the trade press quoted back: *The Stackfield Report* called it "the clearest architecture story in the lakehouse category." This is one of the two launches I mean when I say testing changed what shipped.

## The campaign, in five stages

Each stage had a written exit criterion:

1. **Design-partner program, January to April.** An application page with the tested positioning and a sample dataset to run against, and no screenshots of unfinished features. Exit: 250 registered design partners, 40 running real workloads. Result: 340 registered, 52 running real workloads.
2. **Field enablement, April and May.** A 20-minute story for sellers, three discovery questions, and a one-page "when Ledgerline is the wrong answer." Exit: 70% of the target sales teams certified before launch day. Result: 64%. The gap shows up in the mistakes below.
3. **Launch week, June.** The CEO keynote segment was built around one design partner's before and after: their nightly copy job went from four hours to none. We ran a press embargo with two trade outlets, and the docs and quickstart went live the same hour as the announcement.
4. **Eight-week content sequence, June to August.** One architecture deep-dive a week, each co-written with an engineer and each ending in a runnable example. The advocates ran a live build session every other week.
5. **Paid amplification, June and July.** Paid social and sponsored newsletter placements, deliberately small at 12% of the budget (about $290K).

## The numbers, and their definitions

We agreed the definitions with sales and finance in January, before the first registration:

- **Influenced pipeline:** opportunities sourced by, or touched within 90 days of, a launch-program activity. **$8.2M** in the two quarters after launch.
- **Design partners:** registered through the application page. **340** before GA, 52 of them running real workloads.
- **Launch page to docs:** the share of launch-page visitors who clicked through to the docs in the same session. **71%.** I chose this as the honesty metric. When copy oversells, people bounce off the docs; when it's accurate, they go and read them.
- **Quickstart completion:** 58% of people who started the quickstart finished it in under 15 minutes.

## What didn't work

- **Paid social.** Cost per design-partner registration was about 3 times the organic channels, and fewer than 1 in 10 of those registrations ran a workload. I cut it in week three and moved about $190K of the unspent paid budget into the content sequence. Data engineers didn't want an ad; they wanted to see the architecture.
- **Field enablement landed short.** Two regions hadn't certified by launch day, and their first two weeks of calls used the old "every cloud" pitch. Three prospects asked about a fourth cloud we didn't support. We ran a catch-up session in week two, but by then those deals were already damaged.
- **A sponsored regional conference booth produced zero pipeline.** Under our own definition, nothing touched there converted within 90 days. We stopped buying booths and kept the speaking slots.

## What I'd do differently

- Start field enablement a month earlier, and make certification a launch gate rather than a goal.
- Test the paid channel during the design-partner stage with a small budget, instead of finding out in launch week.
- Put the "when Ledgerline is the wrong answer" page on the public site, not only in the sales kit. Practitioners who found it through sellers trusted the rest of the story more because of it.
