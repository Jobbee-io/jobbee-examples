# Annual operating plan cycle

Quillfield Pet Foods. I've led the plan for FY2025 (built August – December 2024), FY2026 (built August – December 2025), and FY2027, which goes to our board on December 9, 2026. Our fiscal year is the calendar year. On my resume this is one bullet. Most of this file is the FY2025 cycle, my first as lead, because it has a full year of results: the design, the calendar, the revenue build, the gap closed version by version, who disagreed, how the plan did against actuals, and what changed in the next 2 cycles.

## What the plan is, and why it matters here

Our annual plan covers revenue, trade spend, cost of goods, operating expenses, headcount, capital spending and cash. The board approves it in December. Everyone's bonus is measured against it, and our lender's covenant forecasts start from it. When the plan is wrong, people get paid on the wrong number and the lender gets surprises.

The 2 plans before I led missed revenue by −4.2% (FY2023) and −2.1% (FY2024). The FY2024 cycle ran 16 weeks from kickoff to CFO sign-off and went through 7 versions. 14 cost-center owners got Excel templates by email, and the account managers typed revenue in dollars. About 3 weeks went to reconciling templates that didn't add up.

## My role

I owned the calendar, the templates, the consolidation model, the revenue build, the gap analysis between versions, and the first draft of the board book. My director owned sign-off and the trade-offs with the CFO. The CFO presented to the board. The 14 cost-center owners owned their budgets. The supply chain finance analyst owned cost of goods, and the plant finance lead owned capital spending. I had no authority over any of them. I had the calendar and the model.

## How to build revenue: 3 options

- Top-down: spread the target across customers. Fast, but nobody owns an assumption, and the plan turns into a quota.
- Bottom-up dollars from the account managers, the old way. It had missed high 2 years running.
- Driver-based, with sales owning the assumptions (new stores, velocity, promotions, price) and the forecast model turning them into dollars (`Driver Based Forecast Rebuild.md`). This is what I proposed, and my director and the CFO agreed in June 2024.

## The FY2025 design

- Targets first. The CEO and CFO set them before anyone built bottom-up: revenue +7% and an adjusted EBITDA margin of 13.5%, from the private-equity owner's plan.
- A calendar with 4 gates, published in July, each with an owner and a date. Gate 1, August 12: kickoff, targets and the assumptions book out. Gate 2, September 13: the revenue build and the first bottom-up version. Gate 3, October 11: the second version, after round 1 of expense reviews. Gate 4, October 28: CFO sign-off on the third version, 11 weeks after kickoff. The board book went out on November 26 and the board approved the plan on December 10.
- Operating expenses in Anaplan instead of emailed templates, with headcount planned by position: every open and new role with a start date and a cost.
- A one-page assumptions book that every version had to match: price, trade rate, distribution, inflation on our main inputs, wage increase, and the exchange rate for imported ingredients.

## The revenue build, and the 61% conversation

Over FY2021–FY2023, sales delivered 61% of the new distribution they planned: 58%, 66% and 59% by year. My measure was distribution points from new listings live at year end, divided by the distribution points from new listings in that year's plan. I planned new distribution at that rate and kept the rest as a named upside list. That was the hardest conversation of the cycle.

The VP of Sales argued that 61% punished his team for earlier years' misses, and that 3 new listings for FY2025 were already confirmed. Both points were fair. We settled it this way: listings a retailer had confirmed in writing went in at 100%, everything else at 61%, and the gap went on an upside list with an owner for each item, shown to the board, so the team would get credit when an item landed. The CFO backed it. The upside list came to $21M of revenue.

## The gap, version by version

| Version | Date | Revenue | Adjusted EBITDA | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1, bottom-up | September 13 | $679.0M | $78.8M | 11.6% |
| 2 | October 11 | $658.0M | $78.4M | 11.9% |
| 3, signed off | October 28 | $658.0M | $86.0M | 13.1% |

The bridge from version 1 to version 3:
- The 61% rate took $21M out of revenue and about $6.3M out of EBITDA, at about 30% contribution on that revenue after trade, freight and variable costs.
- Round 1 of expense reviews added $5.9M: efficiency asks to every cost-center owner on travel, agencies and consultants, and moving 2 system projects later in the year.
- Round 2 added $7.6M. Most of it was spending planned for sales we were never going to have: retailer marketing for unconfirmed listings, 9 planned field sales and merchandising roles, and extra outside warehouse space.

The final plan had revenue of $658M, +6.0% over FY2024's $621M, against a +7% target of about $664M. Adjusted EBITDA was $86.0M, 13.1%. A 13.5% margin would have been $88.8M, so the plan was $2.8M short. The CFO took it to the board with a $2.8M list of savings we had identified but not put in the plan, because none of them had an owner and a date yet, plus the $21M upside list. The board approved it on the first submission. 3 versions in total.

## The board book

I drafted it alone, as 40 pages of tables. The CFO rewrote the first 3 pages around the 3 decisions the board had to make: accept revenue 1 point under the target, with the upside list tracked on its own line; accept EBITDA $2.8M under the target margin, with the savings list outside the plan; and note the covenant headroom if revenue came in 5% below plan. His version was the right one. Since then I write those decision pages first and build the tables behind them.

## The numbers (FY2025 actual vs plan)

Plan accuracy is (actual − plan) ÷ plan for full-year net revenue. Cycle time is kickoff to CFO sign-off. A version is a full consolidated plan sent to the CFO.

- Revenue $646M, 1.8% below plan. The largest single piece was our largest retailer cutting its inventory in Q3, about $4.5M (`Q3 2025 Forecast Miss.md`). Most of the rest was slower treats velocity. About $4M of the $21M upside list landed.
- Adjusted EBITDA $83.4M, 3.0% below plan.
- Operating expenses 1.4% under plan.
- Cycle: 16 → 11 weeks. Versions: 7 → 3.

## What went wrong

- I sent out the expense templates at kickoff, before the revenue build was finished. Cost-center owners sized their budgets against $679M. When revenue came down to $658M, round 2 was spent cutting spending planned for sales we were never going to have. It cost 2 weeks and some goodwill.
- Headcount planned by position assumed every open role would be filled on its planned start date. On average, roles were filled 2.6 months late. Vacancy was most of the 1.4% expense underspend, and in EBITDA it hid part of the revenue miss. That underspend was predictable, and I didn't plan for it.
- The board book, above.

## Who disagreed, and how it was settled

- The VP of Sales, on the 61% rate. Settled by the confirmed-listings rule and the upside list.
- The head of customer marketing, in round 2. She argued that retailer marketing for unconfirmed listings was what would get them confirmed. We moved $1.2M of it into a fund outside the plan, released only when a retailer confirmed a listing in writing. $0.7M of it was released in FY2025.
- The CFO, on the board book. He was right.

## FY2026 and FY2027

For FY2026 I finished the revenue build before the expense templates went out. I added a 7% vacancy factor on open and new positions. In FY2025 vacancy had saved 8.4% of their planned cost, and I set the factor below that on purpose: too high, and managers can't afford the roles they really need to fill. Revenue got a low, base and high case. The cycle took 10 weeks. Plan revenue was $681M, and through September 2026 we're 0.7% below plan. Both analysts I mentor ran parts of it, one the expense consolidation and the other the headcount module.

FY2027 is on the same calendar. New this year: the low case is built from the weekly tripwire indicators we've tracked since the Q3 2025 miss, so the board's downside case moves on the same 6 indicators that trigger our outlook notes during the year. The board book is in draft now, decision pages first, and goes to the board on December 9.

## What still isn't solved

- Bonuses are set on a single plan number, while the forecast runs on ranges. That gives sales a reason to argue the plan down and gives everyone a reason to treat the base case as a promise. I proposed a payout band around plan for FY2026. The CFO kept the single number because the owner's bonus scheme is built on it.
- The upside list has started to behave like a second plan. Sales talks about it more than about the base case.
- Capital spending is still planned outside the model, by the plant finance lead, and joined to the rest by hand.
