# Medical plan change: the pushback, and what I changed

Orrinvale Health, May to November 2025, for the 2026 plan year. Benefits joined my scope in April 2024, and this was my first large benefits change. On my resume it's one bullet. It's the project I learned the most from, because I got the first version wrong. All figures are plan-level aggregates. I never saw an individual's claims: the broker gave us de-identified reports, with any group under 10 people suppressed.

## The full story

Benefits joined my job in April 2024, and this was my first large benefits change. In May 2025 the broker's actuary projected that our self-funded medical plan would cost 12.6% more in 2026, about $2.7M, and the CFO asked for options that kept the increase near 5%. I owned the cost model and the design proposal. The benefits manager owned the broker, plan administration and open enrollment. The CHRO and CFO decided.

My first proposal was to drop the PPO and move everyone to the high-deductible plan, with a company HSA contribution. It was projected to save $1.6M against trend. It was approved in July and announced at an all-hands on August 12, 2025, as a decision.

I had made two mistakes: I had modeled the average employee, and we announced instead of asking. Within 2 weeks there were 486 comments, a letter signed by 318 employees, and 4 listening sessions that ran out of chairs. I ran 3 of them with the benefits manager. When I reran the model by pay band, employees under $60K would have paid about $1,050 a year more, around 2% of their pay. They had done that math before I did.

I led the redesign. On September 15 we announced it: the PPO stays for 2026 and 2027, contributions are banded by salary, the lowest band gets a larger HSA contribution, maintenance drugs for chronic conditions are covered before the deductible, and the FAQ is written in dollars. Projected savings fell to $0.98M, so the company absorbed about $620K more.

At open enrollment in November 2025, PPO enrollment fell from 44% to 34%, by choice. Plan cost for January to September 2026 is up 6.8%, against 8.0% budgeted.

What's still open: 9 months of claims at our size is noisy, and 2 large claims could still move the full-year number. And the PPO is promised only through 2027. What comes after that is a later decision.

## What it was and why

Orrinvale's medical plan is self-funded: the company pays claims directly, with stop-loss insurance for very large ones. In 2025 we offered 2 plans, a PPO (44% of enrolled employees) and a high-deductible plan with a health savings account (56%). 1,640 employees were enrolled. Total medical and pharmacy cost for 2025 was about $21.4M, about 78% of it paid by the company.

In May 2025 the broker's actuary projected a 12.6% increase for 2026, about $2.7M, driven mostly by specialty drugs and a few large claims. The CFO asked for options that kept the increase near 5%.

## My role

I owned the cost model and the design proposal. The benefits manager owned the broker and vendor relationships, plan administration and open enrollment. The broker's actuary supplied the trend projection and the claims reports by cost tier. The CHRO and CFO decided. After the pushback I led the redesign and ran 3 of the 4 listening sessions with the benefits manager.

## The first proposal

Drop the PPO. Everyone moves to the high-deductible plan, with a company HSA contribution of $750 for single coverage and $1,500 for family. Projected 2026 savings against the 12.6% trend: $1.6M. It was approved in July and announced at an all-hands on August 12, 2025, as a decision.

## The pushback

Within 2 weeks there were 486 comments on the feedback form, a letter signed by 318 employees, and 4 listening sessions that ran out of chairs. Most comments came from 2 groups: Revenue Cycle Operations, where median base pay is about $54K, and people managing chronic conditions on regular medication.

They were right, and my model showed why once I asked it the right question. I had modeled the change on the average employee, whose base pay is about $100K. When I reran it by pay band, using the broker's claims reports by cost tier:

- Employees earning under $60K (29% of those enrolled) would have seen expected out-of-pocket costs rise about $1,050 a year, around 2% of pay.
- Employees earning over $120K would have seen about $390, around 0.3% of pay.
- For anyone with high claims, the single out-of-pocket maximum went from $3,000 to $5,000. For someone earning $48K, that's more than 10% of pay in a bad year.

## What changed

Decided September 9 and announced September 15, 2025:

- The PPO stays for 2026 and 2027.
- Employee contributions are banded by salary: under $60K, $60K to $120K, over $120K. The lowest band pays about 40% less than before for either plan; the highest band pays about 15% more for the PPO.
- The HSA contribution for the lowest band rose to $1,250 single and $2,500 family.
- A list of maintenance drugs for chronic conditions (for example diabetes, blood pressure and asthma) is covered before the deductible on the high-deductible plan.
- The FAQ was rewritten in dollars, with 4 worked examples, instead of percentages and plan jargon.

Projected savings against trend fell from $1.6M to $0.98M. The company absorbed about $620K more than in the first proposal.

## The numbers, with their definitions

- Open enrollment, November 2025: PPO 44% → 34%, high-deductible plan 56% → 66%. People moved by choice, not because a plan disappeared.
- High-deductible enrollees putting their own money into the HSA: 58% → 71%.
- 2026 plan cost, January to September: up 6.8% on the same months of 2025, against 8.0% budgeted for the revised design. 9 months of claims at our size is noisy; 2 large claims can move that number by a point.
- Engagement survey, "I understand the value of my benefits": 61% favorable in 2025, 68% in 2026.

## What went wrong

- I modeled the average employee. A cost-sharing change always lands differently by pay, and I didn't run it by pay band until employees did the math for me.
- We announced a decision instead of testing a proposal. Listening sessions after an announcement feel like damage control, and some of ours were.
- The first FAQ talked about "embedded deductibles" and percentages. Nobody plans their year in percentages.

## What I'd do differently

- Run every option by pay band and claims tier before it goes to the CFO, not after it's announced. That check is now the first step of any benefits change I work on.
- Take 2 or 3 options to employees before deciding, each with its cost to them in dollars.
- Write the FAQ first. If a change can't be explained in dollars to someone earning $48K, it isn't ready.
