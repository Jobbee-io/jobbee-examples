# Job leveling framework: 634 titles to 211 jobs

Orrinvale Health, October 2022 to July 2023. My first large project there, and the base that the pay equity audit, every merit cycle and the promotion guideline sit on. On my resume it's one bullet. This file holds the options I rejected, the design and the arguments over it, how the mapping and the ranges were built, the cost, the appeals, the launch mistakes, and what still isn't solved. All figures are aggregates.

## What it was and why

When I joined in August 2022, 1,812 employees held 634 different job titles, and 41% of those titles were held by one person. There were no levels. About 55% of jobs had a salary range, and those ranges had been set one at a time, whenever someone asked. Two people with the same title could be doing very different work, and two people doing the same work could have titles that sounded a level apart. Managers couldn't explain promotions, and I couldn't price jobs I couldn't define.

## My role

I designed the framework and ran the project. My director sponsored it and chaired the leveling committee: 6 VPs from the largest functions, who approved the level definitions and settled disputes. Managers proposed the mapping for their own people; I reviewed every proposal. I built the salary ranges and the cost model. Our HRIS partner loaded the new jobs into Workday. It took about half my time for 10 months, more during mapping, alongside my first merit cycle.

## Options I rejected

- Buying a ready-made leveling framework from a consulting firm. The quote was about $140K for the license and setup. Its definitions were written for large tech companies and fit our revenue cycle operations jobs badly, and we'd still have had to map every person ourselves. Instead I wrote our own guides and lined each level up with the levels in our 2 salary surveys, so market pricing still matched cleanly.
- A points-factor job evaluation. It's precise, but a manager can't explain a points score to an employee in a one-to-one.
- Broad bands, 6 to 8 wide pay bands with no levels inside. That would have fixed the ranges and left the titles and the career steps as unclear as before.
- Keep pricing jobs one at a time. That was the status quo, and the reason for the project.

## The design

- 3 tracks: professional (P1 to P6), management (M1 to M5), and operations and support (S1 to S4). An individual contributor can reach P6 without managing anyone, so nobody has to become a manager to be paid more.
- Level guides, not points. Each level is defined by scope, complexity, independence and impact, in plain sentences, for each of 14 job families.
- One title per job: P1 Associate, P2 with no prefix, P3 Senior, P4 Staff, P5 Principal, P6 Distinguished. No separate "business titles", because that's how we got to 634.
- 211 jobs. A job is a job family, a level and, where the market pays differently, a specialization (for example, data engineering inside Engineering).
- Ranges: midpoints at the market median from 2 salary surveys, one tech-industry and one healthcare-industry, with survey data aged to July 1, 2023 at 3.5% a year. 168 of the 211 jobs had a direct survey match. The other 43 were slotted between matched jobs in the same family.
- 3 geographic pay zones. Zone 1 ranges sit at 110% of Zone 2 and Zone 3 at 92%. Minneapolis is Zone 2. Remote employees are zoned by home address.
- Width from minimum to maximum: 30% for S levels, 40% for P levels, 50% for M levels. S jobs have a tight market and people move up through levels quickly. Manager pay varies most with the size of the team, so M ranges needed the most room.
- No pay cuts. Anyone above their new maximum is red-circled: base stays where it is, and future increases come as lump sums until the range catches up.

## Who disagreed, and how it was settled

- The VP of Engineering wanted 7 P levels, mostly so that people with senior titles today would map one level higher. With 1,812 employees, 7 levels would have left the top 2 almost empty. The committee chose 6, and agreed that people map by the work in the level guide, not by their current title. That rule is where the launch mistake below came from.
- Engineering also asked for midpoints at the 60th percentile of the market, not the median. The CFO wouldn't fund a lead policy for one function. Instead, the merit cycle got a market-adjustment pot for job families whose market moves faster than our ranges (`Annual Merit Cycle.md`).
- The first draft of the operations and support track had 3 levels. The Revenue Cycle Operations leaders showed me a real 4-step progression in their teams, from specialist to subject-matter expert, and they were right. S4 was added in April, before mapping closed.
- Finance proposed phasing the raises to range minimum over 2 merit cycles. 112 of the 157 employees below minimum were in Revenue Cycle Operations and Support, our lowest-paid jobs. Phasing would have left them under a minimum we had just shown them in writing. The CFO agreed to raise everyone at once, on July 1.

## How the mapping worked

Each manager got a workbook with their people, the level guides for their family, and a column for a one-line reason tied to the guide. Pay wasn't in the workbook. I wanted the level decided by the work before anyone looked at what the person earned.

I reviewed every one of the 1,812 proposals against the guides and sent 22% back for a second look. The 3 common errors: mapping by current title, mapping by current pay ("she's paid like a P4"), and pushing someone up a level to keep them. Then I ran 14 calibration sessions, one per family, with that family's leaders. We compared the family's spread across levels with the typical spread for that family in the surveys, and talked through every level where we looked out of line.

## Ranges and cost

After mapping, the median employee sat at 0.96 of their range midpoint.

- 157 employees (8.7%) were paid under the minimum of their new range. All were raised to the minimum on July 1, 2023: $468K a year, 0.27% of base payroll.
- 61 employees (3.4%) were above their new maximum and were red-circled.
- Every letter showed the employee's job, level and range, plus the ranges one level above and one level below in the same family, so the next step was visible.

One effect I didn't plan for: women were 71% of Revenue Cycle Operations and Support, so the raises to minimum moved pay for a mostly female group 2 months before the pay equity audit started. We had no gap measured before leveling, so I can't say by how much.

## Timeline

1. October to December 2022: level guides drafted with each family's leaders and approved by the committee.
2. January and February 2023: ranges built and costed.
3. March and April: managers mapped their people; I reviewed every mapping; calibration sessions.
4. May 15: every employee received a letter with their job, level and range.
5. May 15 to June 14: the appeal window.
6. June: 3 committee sessions to hear appeals.
7. July 1: raises to range minimum took effect.

## Appeals

94 employees appealed in the 30-day window. For each one I prepared a one-page case: the employee's reasons, the manager's reasons, and the level guide text side by side. 33 (35%) changed after committee review: 28 moved up a level and 5 moved to a different job. About 40% of all appeals were really "I don't understand what a level means", not mapping errors.

## The numbers, with their definitions

- Titles to jobs: 634 titles in use on October 1, 2022, became 211 jobs.
- Below range minimum: 157 employees (8.7%), raised at a cost of $468K a year, 0.27% of base payroll.
- Above range maximum: 61 employees (3.4%), red-circled.
- Appeals: 94 filed; 33 (35%) changed.
- Coverage: 100% of jobs now have a range, up from about 55%.

## What went wrong

- Titles changed on the same day as the letters. 11 engineers who had "Principal" in their title mapped to P4, which is "Staff" in the new titles. They found out from a letter, and some of their managers hadn't talked to them first. Several said it read as a demotion, even though no one's pay went down. 3 of the 11 left within 9 months. I can't say leveling was the only reason, but I wouldn't launch it that way again.
- Manager training came after the letters instead of before. The appeals that were really questions about levels would mostly have been answered by training, and the 33 real ones would have been easier to see.

## What it made possible

- The pay equity audit could compare like with like for the first time: job family by level, by zone (`Pay Equity Audit.md`).
- The merit matrix runs on position in range, which needs a range for every job.
- A promotion now means a level change, so we can count promotions and set a guideline for what they pay (`Promotion Pay Guideline.md`).
- Market pricing since then covers about 60 new or changed jobs a year, each mapped to a family and a level first.

## What's still open

- The management track. A 50% range still holds very different jobs: an M2 can lead 3 people or 30. I've looked at splitting M levels by team size and haven't proposed it yet.
- Moving between tracks. Pay at S4 overlaps with P2, and about 20 people a year ask to move from the S track to the P track. There's no defined path. I wrote a draft rule in 2026; it isn't approved.
- Title as status. The "Principal" problem will come back the next time we change a title. The fix is in how we launch, below.

## What I'd do differently

- Managers talk to every person whose title changes before the letter arrives, with a script and the level guide in hand.
- Keep the old title for 6 months for anyone whose new title reads as a step down, then change it with a conversation, not a letter.
- Train managers on what a level means in March, not June.
