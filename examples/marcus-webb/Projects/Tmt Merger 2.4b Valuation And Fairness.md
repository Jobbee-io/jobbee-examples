# TMT merger valuation and fairness — Corrigan / Aldervane

Companion to `Tmt Merger 2.4b.md`, which has the deal summary, timeline and retention work. This file is the financial-analysis lane I led: the models, the valuation ranges behind the fairness opinion, the ability-to-pay analysis, the synergy build and the fight over it, and the collar decision I'd handle differently.

## The full story

This lane ran the whole length of the deal. After Corrigan's board hired us in April 2018, I built the standalone model from the billing system's subscription records. Next to management's 17% growth case I ran a banker case at 13%, because Corrigan had missed its own plan in 2 of the previous 4 years.

In July, Aldervane bid $2.2B in cash and stock. Corrigan's holders would be taking Aldervane shares, so I ran reverse diligence on Aldervane and built its side of the deal. My ability-to-pay analysis put their ceiling near $2.55B. The MDs negotiated price. My job was to give them that ceiling and the reasoning for each step toward it. Inside a clean team, I built the $95M synergy case from both companies' budgets.

October was the hard month. In two sessions, Aldervane's bankers tried to cut $20M of synergies to move the exchange ratio. The sourcing held, and we gave only on phasing. Once price was agreed at $2.4B, we asked for a collar on the stock. That timing was my mistake. I had modeled the collar, and asking for it only after price was set meant asking when we had the least leverage. Aldervane refused, and our side spent its leverage on the reverse termination fee instead.

In November I took the fairness analyses to the internal committee twice. It made me cut 3 precedents and defend a lower-half multiple with growth. The opinion went to Corrigan's board at signing.

My analysts built the comps and precedents, and my associate drafted the debt schedule. I owned the structure of every model and checked every output before it went into a deck.

Two things went wrong after signing, and each came back to a mistake of mine. Aldervane missed its Q1 2019 bookings guidance, and at close in June its stock was 11% down, about $150M off the stock half. With a collar, the shortfall would have been about $15M. The second mistake was my cross-sell attach rate. I learned later, from Aldervane's public updates, that realized synergies were about $84M two years after close, with cross-sell at about half of plan. My 8% attach rate was too high even after I'd cut it. Nothing in this lane is open now: the deal closed in June 2019, and the two-year synergy result is public. The two lessons, price protection in the first-round letter and a cross-sell haircut based on the buyer's own record, are how I run stock deals.

## The models I owned

1. **Corrigan standalone.** Five years, with revenue built by product line and customer cohort from the billing system's subscription records, not from management's summary. Two cases ran through every analysis. Management's plan assumed 17% growth. Our banker case assumed 13%, because Corrigan had missed its own plan in 2 of the previous 4 years, by 2 to 4 points each time.
2. **Aldervane.** Corrigan's holders were taking $1.4B of Aldervane stock, so we did reverse due diligence: public filings, consensus estimates, one management session, and a review of their model with their finance team.
3. **Combined.** Accretion and dilution, pro forma ownership, leverage, and the synergy phasing.

My analysts built the comps and precedents sets; the associate built the first draft of the debt schedule. I owned the structure of every model and checked every output against its source before it went into a deck.

## Valuation ranges behind the fairness opinion

| Method | Basis | Implied enterprise value |
|---|---|---|
| DCF, management case | 9.5–10.5% discount rate, 3–4% terminal growth | $2.3B–$2.8B |
| DCF, banker case | same inputs, 13% growth | $2.0B–$2.5B |
| Trading comparables | 9 listed vertical-software companies, 6.0x–8.5x next-12-month revenue (about $330M) | $2.0B–$2.8B |
| Precedent transactions | 11 vertical-software deals, 2015–2018, 7.3x–9.8x trailing revenue | $2.1B–$2.8B |

The price, $2.4B or about 8.3x trailing revenue, sat inside every range. It sat in the lower half of the precedents, and that was the hard question.

## The fairness committee

Marbury Cross ran every fairness opinion past an internal committee of three MDs who weren't on the deal. I presented to it twice in November 2018. Three questions decided it:

1. **"Why is a lower-half precedent multiple fair?"** My answer was growth. I regressed trailing revenue multiple on revenue growth across the precedents (R² of about 0.6). At Corrigan's 14% growth the line implied about 7.6x, roughly $2.2B. 8.3x sat above the line. The committee also made me cut 3 of my original 14 precedents: two from before 2015 and one take-private of a distressed company. The range tightened and the answer held.
2. **"Why lead with management's case?"** We didn't. Both cases went into the board book, and the banker-case DCF midpoint, about $2.25B, sat below the price. That supported the opinion.
3. **"What are holders actually receiving?"** $1.4B of the price was stock trading at about 19.5x revenue. The opinion speaks to the consideration being fair from a financial point of view at signing. We added explicit language that it took no view on where Aldervane's stock would trade afterward, and I walked the board through the reverse diligence so that risk was a decision, not a footnote.

The opinion was delivered to Corrigan's board at signing.

## Ability to pay

I built Aldervane's side of the deal as carefully as our own, because the price negotiation ran on it.

- At $2.4B the deal was about 4% dilutive to Aldervane's 2019 non-GAAP EPS, accretive in 2020 with half the cost synergies, and accretive to revenue per share from day one.
- The ceiling: about $2.55B, the most they could pay and stay accretive to 2020 non-GAAP EPS with half the cost synergies, with leverage under 5x.
- Their first-round $2.2B left about $350M of room under that ceiling. The MDs negotiated to $2.4B. My job was to give them the ceiling and the reasoning behind each step toward it.

## The synergy build

$95M of annual run-rate synergies, built bottom-up by cost center from both companies' 2018 budgets. Competitively sensitive data went only to a named clean team, of which I was one.

| Item | Run rate | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| R&D | $32M | Consolidating the two overlapping product lines onto one code base each: about 110 fewer roles over 2 years, mostly through attrition and contractor exits, plus tooling |
| Sales and marketing | $19M | Overlapping field coverage in the two shared product lines; one marketing budget instead of two |
| G&A | $18M | Duplicate finance, HR and legal; two ERPs into one; sponsor-reporting and audit costs |
| Hosting | $11M | Corrigan's own data centers moved onto Aldervane's cloud contract |
| Cost subtotal | $80M | About 21% of the combined cost base |
| Cross-sell, both directions | $15M | $21M of revenue at a 70% contribution margin; attach rate 8%, against 11% and 14% for Corrigan's own last two modules after 24 months |
| Total | $95M | |

One-time cost to achieve: about $110M over 2 years, including the $14M retention pool. The R&D line and the cross-sell line, $47M together, are the ones that depended on the 40 people in the retention work.

## The $20M fight

In two sessions in October 2018, Aldervane's bankers argued for $75M, not $95M: take the $15M cross-sell out entirely ("revenue synergies aren't synergies until they're revenue") and cut $5M from sales and marketing. The point was the exchange ratio. The less value the combination created, the less stock they could justify handing Corrigan's holders.

My defense was sourcing. Every cross-sell attach rate came from Corrigan's own module history, cited line by line, and I'd used a lower rate than either of its last two modules. The sales and marketing number came from named overlapping territories, not a percentage.

The outcome: $95M stayed in both boards' materials, and the exchange ratio didn't move. We conceded phasing. Cross-sell was modeled to reach run rate in year 3 instead of year 2. That cost about $25M of present value in our synergy-sharing analysis and nothing in price. It was a give on timing, not on size.

What happened after: Aldervane's public updates put realized run-rate synergies at about $84M two years after close. Costs ran slightly ahead of plan; cross-sell came in at about half of plan. Their bankers were half right about the piece they attacked, and my attach rate was too high even after I'd cut it. I now haircut revenue synergies by the buyer's own cross-sell track record, not only the seller's.

## The collar decision

The stock half of the price created the decision I'd handle differently. I modeled three structures:

- **A fixed exchange ratio.** Corrigan's holders carry every move in Aldervane's price between signing and close.
- **A fixed value with a ±10% collar.** The ratio floats inside the band to deliver $1.4B of stock, and fixes outside it.
- **A fixed ratio with a walk-away right** if Aldervane's stock fell more than 20% and underperformed a software index by 15%.

We raised the collar only after price was agreed in October. Aldervane refused: its board wouldn't issue more shares if its price fell. The sponsor preferred the fixed ratio for the upside. Our side spent its remaining leverage on a $120M regulatory reverse termination fee (5% of deal value) instead, which mattered more once the second request arrived.

Then Aldervane missed its Q1 2019 bookings guidance. At close its shares were 11% below the signing reference price, so the stock half was worth about $1.25B, about $150M less than announced. With the collar, the shortfall would have been about $15M.

Choosing the termination fee over the collar was defensible on the day. Asking at the wrong moment wasn't. Price protection belongs in the first-round letter, as a condition of any stock mix, while the buyer still wants the deal more than it wants to win the point. That is how I run stock deals now.
