# Fintech Series D — Tollbrook Payments, $180M sole-placement round

Marbury Cross, August to December 2020. I led this round below MD: positioning, investor outreach, and the term-sheet fight that defined the deal. A $180M growth round for a payments-infrastructure company, placed with a single growth fund at the company's insistence. That insistence is why the term-sheet fight happened, and it's the most instructive negotiation I've run.

## The company and the market moment

Tollbrook Payments: B2B payments infrastructure, payables automation for mid-market companies. About $38M of ARR growing 90% a year, with a gross-margin story complicated by a revenue share with its banking partner. A second-time founder-CEO. The CFO was a former banker, which made the process faster and the negotiation harder: he knew every trick because he'd run them.

Late 2020, the private fintech market was running hot ahead of the 2021 peak. Tollbrook had inbound interest from five funds and, more importantly, two Series E precedents in its exact category at very high multiples. The CEO wanted a round priced against those precedents, and the market said he'd get it.

The team: me, one associate and two analysts. Our MD owned the CEO relationship and joined the final negotiating session.

## The structure the CEO insisted on

Sole placement: one fund, the whole round. His reasoning, stated in our first meeting and correct: a $180M round from one fund buys a partner with real ownership, and the company's next event, an IPO-track round or a sale, benefits from a lead with enough ownership to act like it matters.

My job was to stop a sole placement from becoming a negotiation with one counterparty at one price.

- **Parallel processes.** 8 funds contacted (the 5 inbound plus 3 we added), 5 taken to diligence depth, and 2 taken to final term-sheet depth at the same time: the eventual lead and one shadow. The CEO never told anyone anything misleading. The structure just meant the lead's term sheet wasn't the only one in the room on pricing day.
- **Financing pre-wired.** The round included a $40M debt facility from Tollbrook's existing bank partner. We priced its covenant package early, so the equity negotiation couldn't be held hostage by financing terms at the end.

Timeline: mandate in August, outreach in September, management meetings and diligence in October, term sheets in early November, three negotiating sessions over 10 days in mid-November, closed in December.

## The term-sheet fight

The lead fund's first term sheet: $180M at a pre-money of about $1.0B, roughly 14x forward ARR, with a clean 1x non-participating preference and pro-rata rights. Also a full-ratchet anti-dilution provision dressed as standard, and a pay-to-play clause with a hair trigger.

The fight was over the ratchet. On a high-multiple round, full ratchet is a time bomb: one down round resets the fund's conversion price all the way to the new price and vaporizes the common.

The CFO and I modeled it in three downside scenarios. The worst was a 2022 round 40% below the Series D price, with a 5-point option-pool top-up. In that case the ratchet plus the top-up moved about 9 percentage points of fully diluted ownership from existing holders to the new fund, before anyone had judged performance.

At first the CEO was inclined to accept it to protect the headline valuation. The 9 points changed his mind.

The fund's argument: every deal at this multiple carries a ratchet. Our answer: neither category precedent did. Both companies' charters, publicly filed in Delaware, showed broad-based weighted-average anti-dilution.

The resolution took three sessions across ten days:

- The fund dropped the ratchet for broad-based weighted average. In exchange we accepted a pay-to-play provision, rewritten to the standard trigger (it bites only in a down round, on holders who don't take their pro rata), where we had wanted it struck entirely.
- Valuation held flat. The number never moved, which mattered for the story the CEO would tell his company.
- **The give I'd revisit:** registration rights more investor-favorable than market, 3 demand registrations instead of 2, exercisable after 3 years instead of 5. We conceded it late in session three, when everyone wanted the deal done. Small dollars, but a precedent: I've since watched that clause get cited in a later round's negotiation. The last session of a deal is where discipline goes to die, and "it's just registration rights" is how it sounds when it happens.

## What the round bought, and how it was told

- $180M primary at about 14x forward ARR, a post-money of about $1.18B, about 15% to the fund. Priced at the top of the market without being the top of it.
- Use of proceeds was the spine of the positioning. Not "growth at all costs" but three named investments: diversifying banking partners (the gross-margin fix), two product lines, and building an international entity, each with a plan the board could measure. The fund underwrote the plan, not the multiple, and that's why the multiple survived the ratchet fight.
- The positioning memo I wrote, "the payments layer beneath the payments layer," told the revenue-quality story honestly: the bank revenue share named, with the diversification plan next to it. In a market full of rounds sold on multiples, the honesty was the differentiation. Two funds that lost the deal cited the memo as why they'd wanted in.

## Aftermath

The fund's partner and the CEO built a genuinely productive board relationship. I checked in quarterly for my remaining time at Marbury Cross; follow-through is a habit and a lead source.

The 2022 repricing hit the category. Tollbrook's round was priced at the top but structured clean enough (weighted average, no ratchet, standard pay-to-play) that the repricing was survivable rather than fatal. The company was sold in 2024 at a price below the Series D post-money but above every preference stack's trigger, so common holders got a soft landing instead of a wipeout. The structure fight in 2020 is a large part of why.

## What I'd do differently

- Pull the precedents' public charters before the first term sheet arrives, not in response to it. We'd have opened with the market evidence instead of finding it under pressure.
- Put every remaining open point on one page before the last session and agree in advance what we won't trade. Registration rights got traded because nobody had written them down as a line.
