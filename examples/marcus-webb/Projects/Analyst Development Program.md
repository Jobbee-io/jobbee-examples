# Analyst development program — rebuilding training and staffing

Halstead Partners, 2022–2023. I designed and ran the overhaul of analyst training and staffing at a 60-person M&A boutique after two consecutive exit-survey cycles showed the same failure modes. Twelve-month analyst attrition fell from 31% to 14%. This is a human-systems project at senior finance level — no models, no deals, and more organizational-design learning than anything else I've done.

## What was broken, diagnosed honestly

Halstead ran a class of 8–10 analysts a year with training that had accreted over a decade: a two-week onboarding (a modeling template walkthrough and "shadow your team"), then sink-or-swim. Exit interviews from the two prior cycles said, with painful consistency:

1. **"Nobody told me what the work was for."** Analysts built schedules without seeing the analysis they fed. Feedback loops were long or absent.
2. **Staffing was a black box.** Assignments arrived by message from a partner's EA. Good analysts got *more* work as their reward, and the staffing logic ("the MD you know gets the staffing") was invisible and unfalsifiable from below.
3. **The model review was the only teaching moment, and it was punitive.** Red ink at 11pm, no standards doc, every reviewer with different expectations. Juniors couldn't tell whether they were learning or being hazed.
4. **The 31% twelve-month attrition concentrated in months 9–14** — after bonus cycle, before any visible path. The surveys' own summary sentence: "I can't see what year two buys me."

Total cost when I pitched the fix: each analyst departure cost a replacement class plus 6+ months of senior-banker training time. Attrition was the most expensive "we've always done it this way" at the firm.

## The program — four components, each aimed at one failure

**1. Training: a real curriculum, six weeks, with graded work products.** Rebuilt as three blocks — modeling standards (one firm-wide template with a written standards doc; the LBO module my old co-trainer and I argued about is now two modules instead of one), process craft (data room management, Q&A logs, diligence trackers — taught by the juniors who were actually good at it, which was itself a signal change), and client communication (how to draft a banker's email, taught with real redlines from real deals, anonymized). Every block ends in a work product reviewed against the standards doc, not against the reviewer's mood.

**2. Staffing: visibility over (claimed) perfection.** I didn't try to make staffing meritocratic by fiat — that fight isn't winnable and the claim isn't even true at good firms. I made it *legible*: a weekly staffing sheet visible to all bankers showing who's staffed on what, at what loading, with open assignments posted before they're privately offered. No rules changed about who can ask for whom; the change was that the asks happened in a visible market instead of in dark corners. The behavioral shift was faster than any mandate would have been: once loading was visible, the worst-staffing offenders self-corrected because their names were on the sheet next to the numbers.

**3. Feedback: the 48-hour rule.** Every work product gets written feedback within 48 hours, against the standards doc, signed by the reviewer. Not faster-always — *bounded* feedback, because unbounded feedback that arrives whenever the deal allows is functionally no feedback. Partners grumbled for one review cycle; then one of them used the format to win an argument with another partner about a model, and the grumbling lost its constituency.

**4. The year-two map.** A published, honest description of what year two buys: which skills, which client exposure, what the promotion decision actually reads (I asked the three most recent promotees what got them promoted and wrote *that* down, not the official criteria). The surveys' "what does year two buy me" died when the answer was a page instead of a shrug.

## Results

- **Twelve-month analyst attrition: 31% → 14%** (two post-launch cycles; small numbers, honest caveat — n≈10 per class, so the confidence interval is wide, but two cycles in the same direction with exit-survey sentiment flipping is signal).
- Exit interviews stopped containing "nobody told me what the work was for" entirely — the sentence I'd declared the program's nemesis.
- Secondary effects I hadn't designed for: the standards doc got adopted upward (associates started handing it to VPs as the review rubric — a junior-authored document disciplining senior review), and two competitors' analysts cited the program's public artifacts in their interviews with us.
- What didn't work, kept in the record: my first version of the staffing sheet included "utilization scores" per banker. It created gaming within three weeks (defensive under-loading before planned vacations, visible-but-meaningless client calls). I removed scoring and kept visibility; legibility without leaderboards is the version that survives contact with humans.

## What it taught me about the senior job

- **Training is a deal-quality product.** The error rate in analyst work products dropped visibly after the curriculum; the partners who'd dismissed "training time" as overhead stopped within a quarter.
- **Legibility beats mandate, every time.** I changed no staffing rule and moved the behavior — the pattern I've since applied to every process problem I own.
- **Programs die without an owner with fee income.** I staff on deals and run this program; the day it becomes "the HR thing" it dies. That's why I keep it, and why I'd bring it with me to a platform — with the caveat that I'd rebuild it for their scale rather than transplant it, because transplanting programs is how you get the utilization-score mistake again.
