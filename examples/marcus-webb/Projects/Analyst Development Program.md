# Analyst development program — rebuilding training and staffing

Halstead Partners, 2022–2023, still running. I designed and ran the overhaul of analyst training and staffing at a 60-person M&A boutique, after two exit-survey cycles in a row showed the same failure modes. Twelve-month analyst attrition fell from 31% to 14%. I did it alongside a full deal load; one managing partner sponsored it.

## What was broken

Halstead hires a class of 8 to 10 analysts a year. Training had accreted over a decade: two weeks of onboarding (a modeling-template walkthrough and "shadow your team"), then sink or swim. Exit interviews from the 2020 and 2021 classes said the same four things:

1. **"Nobody told me what the work was for."** Analysts built schedules without seeing the analysis they fed. Feedback came late or not at all.
2. **Staffing was a black box.** Assignments arrived by message from a partner's assistant. Good analysts were rewarded with more work, and the real logic ("the MD you know gets the staffing") was invisible and impossible to challenge from below.
3. **Model review was the only teaching moment, and it was punitive.** Red ink at 11pm, no standards doc, and every reviewer expecting something different. Juniors couldn't tell whether they were learning or being hazed.
4. **Attrition clustered in months 9 to 14:** after the bonus, before any visible path. The surveys' own summary: "I can't see what year two buys me."

The baseline: 5 of the 16 analysts in the 2020 and 2021 classes left within 12 months of their start date, 31%. Our own estimate of a departure's cost was about $150K: search fees, the first-year training investment, and roughly 200 hours of associate and VP time to train a replacement. That's what I pitched to the managing partners in March 2022: attrition was the most expensive "we've always done it this way" at the firm.

## The program: four parts, each aimed at one failure

**1. Training: six weeks, with graded work products.** Three blocks. Modeling standards: one firm-wide template with a written standards doc. The LBO module my old Marbury Cross co-trainer and I used to argue about is now two modules instead of one. Process craft: data rooms, Q&A logs, diligence trackers, taught by the juniors who were actually good at it, which was itself a signal. Client communication: how to draft a banker's email, taught with real redlines from real deals, anonymized. Every block ends in a work product reviewed against the standards doc, not the reviewer's mood. First class: July 2022.

**2. Staffing: visible, not claimed to be perfect.** I didn't try to make staffing meritocratic by decree. That fight isn't winnable, and the claim isn't true even at good firms. I made it legible: a weekly staffing sheet, visible to every banker, showing who's on what at what loading, with open assignments posted before they're privately offered. No rule changed about who can ask for whom. The asks just happened in a visible market instead of in corners. Live from September 2022. Once loading was visible, the worst offenders corrected themselves, because their names were on the sheet next to the numbers.

**3. Feedback: the 48-hour rule.** Every work product gets written feedback within 48 hours, against the standards doc, signed by the reviewer. Bounded, not instant, because feedback that arrives whenever the deal allows is functionally no feedback. Partners grumbled for one review cycle. Then one of them used the format to win an argument with another partner about a model, and the grumbling lost its constituency.

**4. The year-two map.** A published, honest page on what year two buys: which skills, which client exposure, and what the promotion decision actually reads. I asked the three most recent promotees what got them promoted and wrote that down, not the official criteria. "What does year two buy me" disappeared from the surveys once the answer was a page instead of a shrug.

## What went wrong first

My first staffing sheet included a utilization score for each banker. It created gaming within three weeks: defensive under-loading before planned vacations, and visible but pointless client calls. I removed the scores in October 2022 and kept the visibility. Legibility without a leaderboard is the version that survives contact with people.

## Results

- **Twelve-month attrition: 31% to 14%.** 3 of the 21 analysts in the 2022 and 2023 classes left within 12 months. With classes of about 10 the confidence interval is wide, but two classes in the same direction, with exit-survey sentiment flipping, is signal.
- "Nobody told me what the work was for" stopped appearing in exit interviews entirely.
- **Fewer errors.** I sampled 20 models reviewed before the program and 20 after: the median number of first-review comments went from 38 to 14. The partners who'd called training time overhead stopped within a quarter.
- **Effects I hadn't designed for.** The standards doc got adopted upward: associates started handing it to VPs as the review rubric, a junior-authored document disciplining senior review. And candidates from two competitors cited the year-two map, which we give to recruits, in their interviews with us.

## What it taught me about the senior job

- **Training is a deal-quality product,** and the review-comment numbers are how I'd argue that to any partner who calls it overhead.
- **Legibility beats mandate.** I changed no staffing rule and the behavior moved. I've used the same pattern on every process problem I've owned since.
- **A program dies without an owner who earns fees.** I staff deals and run this program; the day it becomes "the HR thing," it dies. I'd bring it to a larger platform, but rebuilt for that platform's scale rather than transplanted. Transplanting a program is how you get the utilization-score mistake again.
