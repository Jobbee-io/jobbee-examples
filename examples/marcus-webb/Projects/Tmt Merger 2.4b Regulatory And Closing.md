# TMT merger regulatory and closing — Corrigan / Aldervane

Companion to `Tmt Merger 2.4b.md`, which has the deal summary, timeline and retention work. This file covers the stretch from signing to close: the second request and the economic work behind it, the outside date, the buyer's financing, funds flow, and the working-capital peg.

## The terms that framed it

Set at signing in November 2018, negotiated by the MDs and M&A counsel:

- **Outside date** 6 months after signing, mid-May 2019, with one automatic 3-month extension if only antitrust conditions were still open.
- **A $120M regulatory reverse termination fee** (5% of deal value), payable by Aldervane if the deal failed on antitrust grounds.
- **A divestiture cap.** Aldervane didn't have to divest any product line with more than $20M of revenue. Both overlapping lines, resident payments and maintenance work orders, were above that. A divestiture demand on either would have let Aldervane walk away by paying the fee, and the synergy math and the financing would have moved with it.

The HSR filing went in late November 2018.

## The second request

It arrived in late December 2018, about 6 weeks after signing. The agency's concern was the two overlapping lines. Antitrust counsel ran the legal response and the agency relationship. I ran the economic support: the market-definition and share analyses behind counsel's white paper, and preparing Corrigan's business leads for interviews on how their market worked.

The agency's starting theory used the narrowest market it could draw: maintenance software for residential portfolios over 1,000 units. Under that definition, the combined share was 17%. Our honest answer was that the two companies held under 10% each in adjacent but distinct segments. My job was to make that answer defensible, not just stated.

Combined shares under each definition we put in front of the agency:

| Product | Market definition | Combined share |
|---|---|---|
| Work orders | All property-maintenance software, residential and commercial | 6% |
| Work orders | Residential only | 9% |
| Work orders | Residential portfolios over 1,000 units (the agency's starting point) | 17% |
| Resident payments | All rent and resident payment processing | 4% |
| Resident payments | Payments integrated into property-management software | 8% |

Why the narrow definition didn't hold: switching data. Both companies' CRMs held about 2,900 competitive opportunities over 3 years, with the competitor recorded. The two met head to head in 7% of them. In the large-portfolio segment the most-cited alternatives were horizontal maintenance tools and in-house systems, not each other.

The sources, named per cell in the appendix: a third-party survey of property managers that we bought (unit counts and software used), the two CRMs, competitors' public filings, and customer counts from public case lists. I built the appendix so any share could be recomputed under any definition the agency proposed, with the sensitivity to each assumption shown. The antitrust partner's one-line review of it, "this will hold," remains among the more valued sentences of my career.

The production was about 40,000 documents, most of them emails, from 22 custodians. Counsel and a review vendor did the document review; I didn't. Substantial compliance was certified in late April 2019. The waiting period expired in late May with no action and no divestiture conditions.

The mistake: the overlap was obvious at signing, and we started the market-definition work only when the request arrived. The first three weeks went into rebuilding win/loss data that had sat in the two CRMs all along.

## The outside date

The waiting period was always going to end after mid-May, so the automatic extension kicked in and the outside date moved to mid-August. Nobody had to negotiate it, which is the point of writing the extension in at signing. Aldervane never asked to renegotiate on the strength of the delay.

## The buyer's financing

Aldervane paid the $1.0B cash portion with $250M of its own cash and a $750M term loan committed by two banks. There was no financing condition. Financing certainty was a scored criterion in our bid evaluation, so I'd checked the commitment papers and the leverage before signing: just under 5x pro forma EBITDA, counting about $116M of combined EBITDA plus half of the $80M cost synergies.

Aldervane's treasury hedged the loan's rate with a deal-contingent swap that ran to the original outside date. The extension meant extending the hedge, at a cost of about $3M. Aldervane's counsel floated sharing it. We said no: under the agreement, financing cost was the buyer's risk. It was settled in one call.

## Funds flow, built three times

I rebuilt the funds-flow model for three closing dates: May 31 (if the waiting period had ended early), June 28 (what happened) and August 15 (the extended outside date). Each version carried cash per share and per option, the sponsor's proceeds, the payoff of Corrigan's $210M term loan, transaction fees, the cash part of the retention pool, and an estimate of the working-capital adjustment.

The close date mattered because of how Corrigan billed. Most customers paid annually in January, so deferred revenue peaked in Q1 and ran down through the year. The peg was a trailing 12-month average of net working capital. A close in May, June or August sat at a different point on that curve, and the expected adjustment moved with it. A peg dispute plus a moving close date plus a hedged financing is a schedule-risk problem wearing a spreadsheet costume.

## The working-capital peg

The dispute was deferred revenue.

- **Aldervane's position:** count all deferred revenue at face value as a current liability. Lower working capital, bigger price reduction.
- **Ours:** the real obligation is the cost of delivering the service, about 25% of face value at Corrigan's cost of revenue.

Across both treatments and the three dates, I'd modeled a price reduction of $6M to $12M. It settled at $8.2M on the final weekend: face value for deferred revenue on contracts billed in the 90 days before close, cost to serve for the rest. The last session ran 14 hours on the Saturday before close: the peg, the final funds-flow tie-out, and every wire instruction checked twice. The deal closed on June 28, 2019.

## Who owned what

- **Me:** the economic support for the second request, the financing-certainty check, funds flow, the peg model and its numbers in the negotiation.
- **Antitrust counsel:** legal strategy, the white paper, the agency relationship.
- **M&A counsel:** the agreement, including the definitions the peg fight turned on.
- **The MDs:** the termination fee, outside date and divestiture cap at signing.

## What I'd do differently

- Start market definition at signing whenever there is product overlap, with the CRM win/loss pull as step one.
- Get the deferred-revenue treatment written into the agreement's working-capital definition at signing. We left it to the closing statement, and it cost a weekend and a range of $6M of uncertainty that a sentence could have removed.
