# TMT merger — Corrigan Software / Aldervane Systems ($2.4B)

Marbury Cross, 2018–2019, sell side. I was the VP who led the financial-analysis workstream on a $2.4B software merger through a second-request regulatory snag and the retention fight that followed. This is a deal story in banker terms — workstream map, the regulatory month, and the retention drama — because investment banking projects are projects too, and `Projects/` isn't only for engineers.

## The deal

Corrigan Software (vertical SaaS for property management, ~$290M revenue, founder-CEO, PE sponsor holding 40%) agreed to merge with Aldervane Systems (complementary vertical, ~$210M revenue, strategic acquirer). Structure: cash-plus-stock merger, announced value $2.4B. Sell side, two-bidder process that produced one buyer and one stalking-horse dynamic we never had to use.

My workstream map — four lanes, me on all four, one associate and two analysts under me:

1. **Financial analysis lane:** the merger model (standalone + combined, accretion/dilution, synergies bottom-up by function), the fairness-supporting analyses, and the board-deck financials. The synergy model was the contested document of the deal — see below.
2. **Regulatory lane:** HSR support — the second request came, and the regulatory month happened. Coordination with antitrust counsel on the production of competitive-overlap documents: two product lines where the acquirer and target competed head-to-head became a document-review exercise of ~40K emails.
3. **Retention lane:** key-employee retention analysis — who the combined company had to keep for the synergies to be real, what it would cost, and (the hard part) who had to be told what, and when.
4. **Process lane:** data room second-stage, buyer diligence coordination, and the closing-mechanics grind — funds flow, the working-capital peg dispute, and the 14-hour final weekend that always exists.

## The regulatory month

The second request arrived six weeks after signing. The acquirer's two overlapping product lines were the risk: if the agencies forced a divestiture of either, the synergy math and the financing moved.

What the month looked like from my seat:

- Counsel ran the legal response; I ran the **economic support**: market-share analyses of the overlapping products (the honest answer was "under 10% share each in adjacent-but-distinct segments," and my job was to make that answer *defensible*, not just stated — segmentation definitions, share sources, and the sensitivity of the answer to each definition).
- The deal-timeline consequence was real: the second request pushed the outside date once. I rebuilt the funds-flow model three times against three possible closing dates, which is boring to write and was not boring to do — a peg dispute plus a moving close date plus a financing with a rate lock is a schedule-risk problem wearing a spreadsheet costume.
- Resolution: clean second-request expiration with no divestiture conditions, on the strength of the segmentation work. The antitrust partner's one-line review of my market-share appendix ("this will hold") remains among the more valued sentences of my career.

## The retention drama

The synergy model committed to $95M of annual run-rate synergies, and a meaningful share of it sat on the backs of roughly 40 named people: the two overlapping product organizations. The retention analysis I ran (with HR) identified the 40, priced retention packages, and produced the uncomfortable finding that two of the names were executives who would learn about the merger's structure from this process.

The drama, in three beats:

1. **The leak-shaped-accident.** During second-stage diligence, the buyer's integration lead asked a diligence question that could only be answered by naming one of the 40. Counsel caught it in the Q&A review; I drafted the redline that answered the question without the name. The lesson that stuck: in a merger, a diligence question is a document, and every document is a retention risk.
2. **The founder's ask.** The founder-CEO wanted to tell his key people early — a noble instinct that would have put 40 confidentiality walls at risk pre-announcement. I built him the alternative: a pre-signed retention framework (board-approved, unexecuted) that let him make a *credible* promise without revealing names or terms. He took it. The promise he made was the framework; nobody else needed to know that a spreadsheet had saved the announcement.
3. **The one that left anyway.** Retention closed at 37 of 40 at announcement; one of the three departures was the head of the overlapping product line, who resigned six weeks post-close. The postmortem read: we priced his package right and his *manager's* support wrong. I've run retention analysis with a "manager of the retained" column ever since.

## What I actually owned

The merger model and every number in the board deck; the synergy model's bottom-up build (and its defense in two buyer negotiations where their bankers tried to cut $20M of synergies to shrink the stock consideration's justification); the regulatory economic-support package; the retention analysis and framework; the funds-flow and peg resolution. My associate ran the data room; my analysts were the best two I've worked with; the MDs sold the deal. That division of labor is the honest shape of a VP seat at its best.

## Numbers, defensible

- $2.4B announced value; $95M committed run-rate synergies (bottom-up, function-by-function — I can rebuild it from memory).
- ~40K documents produced in the second request; zero divestiture conditions.
- Retention: 37/40 at announcement; the retention framework reused as the template on two later deals I ran.
- One outside-date extension, one peg dispute settled at $8.2M against a $6–12M modeled range (the model was right to a remarkable and slightly annoying degree).
