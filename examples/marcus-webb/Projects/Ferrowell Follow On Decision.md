# Ferrowell follow-on decision — the partial pass I'd make again

Ferrowell Capital, 2015, about four weeks of work. On the resume this is half of one bullet: "modeled and defended two follow-on decisions; one worked, one I got wrong for the right reasons." This is the one I got wrong. I don't name the company: it was private fund business.

## The position

An enterprise-software company selling workflow software to mid-size insurers, where I was Ferrowell's board observer. Ferrowell had led its Series B in 2013: $25M for about 18%.

In 2015 another firm led a $60M Series C at a $420M post-money. Our pro rata was about $9M. At the Series C price our stake was marked at about $65M, about 14% of the fund's NAV, and already the largest position in the fund. Our policy sent anything above 10% of NAV to the investment committee. Full pro rata would have taken it to about 16%.

## The options and my call

- **Full pro rata, $9M.** The deal partner's view: the company was winning, and we knew it better than any new investor.
- **Zero.** The fund CFO's view: we were already overweight.
- **$3M, a third of pro rata.** My recommendation, in the IC memo.

My model of the follow-on dollars, over 4 years: base case 2.4x (55% weight), upside 5x (15%), downside 0.7x (30%), the downside protected by a 1x non-participating preference. That's about 2.3x expected, below the 3x gross bar the fund used for new positions. On top of a position that already carried the fund's biggest single-company risk, the full $9M added concentration without enough return to pay for it. $3M kept us visibly supportive and kept our seat at the table.

The IC approved $3M.

## What happened

In 2018 the company sold to a strategic buyer for $1.5B, about 3.6x the Series C post-money. The $6M we didn't invest would have returned about $21M: roughly $15M of gain missed, about 2% of the fund's committed capital. I'd left Ferrowell by then and watched it from the outside.

## What I got wrong inside the right call

The sizing logic was right. One input was wrong: the 15% upside weight. The sale was driven by a platform rewrite that let the company sell to large carriers, and that rewrite was on the roadmap I had reviewed as board observer. I discounted it because the previous rewrite had slipped a year. I weighted the team's past slip more than the evidence that it had fixed the cause: a new CTO, hired 9 months earlier, who had already shipped two releases on time.

With a 30% upside weight, taken from the base case, the expected multiple is about 2.7x: still under the bar, still on top of the concentration. I'd have recommended about $5M instead of $3M. Same shape, bigger number. That's why I say I'd make the same call with the same information, and why I don't claim the outcome proves the call was right.

The other follow-on decision, in 2014, was full pro rata on a smaller position. It returned 3.1x.

## What I carry into banking

"Is this a good investment" and "is this a good investment for this fund" are different questions. On every sell-side process with sponsor bidders, I map each fund's size, vintage and room in its concentration limits before the first round. It explains bids that otherwise look irrational, and it tells me which bidder can actually stretch.
