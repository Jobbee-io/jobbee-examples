# Fintech Series D — Ledgerline Payments, $180M sole-placement round

Marbury Cross, 2020. I led this round below MD: positioning, investor outreach, and the term-sheet fight that defined the deal. A $180M growth round for a payments-infrastructure company, placed with a single growth fund at the company's insistence — which is the whole reason the term-sheet fight happened and the most instructive negotiation I've run.

## The company and the market moment

Ledgerline Payments: B2B payments infrastructure (payables automation for mid-market companies), ~$38M ARR growing 90% year-over-year, gross-margin story complicated by a banking-partner revenue share. Founder-CEO, second-time; CFO was a former banker, which made the process faster and the negotiation harder — he knew every trick because he'd run them.

The timing was late 2020: the private-fintech market was running hot ahead of the 2021 peak. Ledgerline had inbound interest from five funds and, more importantly, two Series E precedents in their exact category at eye-watering multiples. The CEO wanted a round that priced against those precedents; the market said he'd get it. This is where the deal got interesting.

## The structure the CEO insisted on

**Sole placement.** One fund, whole round. His reasoning (stated to me in the first meeting, correctly): a $180M round from one fund buys a real partner with real ownership, and the company's *next* round — an IPO-track round or an M&A exit — benefits from a lead with enough ownership to act like it matters.

My job was to make a sole placement not be a negotiation with one counterparty at one price. The design:

- **Parallel_processes discipline:** two funds taken to final-term-sheet depth simultaneously (the chosen lead and one shadow), plus three at diligence depth. The CEO communicated nothing misleading; the structure just ensured the chosen fund's term sheet wasn't the only term sheet in the room on pricing day.
- **Pre-wired financing assurance:** the round included a debt facility from Ledgerline's existing bank partner; we priced its covenant package early so the equity negotiation couldn't be held hostage by financing terms late.

## The term-sheet fight

The lead fund's first term sheet: $180M at a pre-money implying ~14x forward ARR, with a **1x non-participating preference** (clean), pro-rata rights — and a **ratchet-based anti-dilution** provision dressed as standard, plus a **pay-to-play clause with a hair trigger**.

The fight was over the ratchet. Full-ratchet anti-dilution on a high-multiple round is a time bomb: one down-round event and it vaporizes the common. The CFO and I modeled what the ratchet did in three downside scenarios — the honest modeling showed that in the worst case, the ratchet plus the option pool top-up transfered ~9 points of economics from existing holders to the new fund *before* any performance judgment. The fund's argument: every deal at this multiple carries it. Our answer, backed by the two category precedents' public term sheets: **neither of them did** — both closed with broad-based weighted-average anti-dilution.

The resolution took three sessions across ten days:

- We dropped the ratchet (to weighted-average) in exchange for conceding the pay-to-play's trigger threshold to a more standard formulation.
- We held valuation flat — the number never moved, which mattered for the story the CEO would tell the company.
- The give we made that I'd revisit: a **registration-rights aggressive-ness** (demand-registration count and timing) that was more founder-favorable than market, conceded late in session three when everyone wanted the deal done. Small dollars, precedent-setting rights; I've since watched that concession get cited in a later round's negotiation. Lesson: the last session of a deal is where discipline goes to die, and "it's just registration rights" is how it sounds when it happens.

## What the round bought and how it was told

- $180M primary, sole placement, priced ~14x forward ARR at the top of the market without being the top of it.
- Use of proceeds was the positioning spine: not "growth at all costs" — three named infrastructure investments (bank-partner diversification — the gross-margin fix —, two product lines, international entity build) with a board-measurable plan attached. The fund underwrote the plan, not the multiple; that's why the multiple survived the ratchet fight.
- The positioning memo I wrote — "the payments layer beneath the payments layer" — leaned on the revenue-quality story we told honestly (the bank revenue share, named, with the diversification plan attached). In a market full of rounds being sold on multiples, the honesty was the differentiation. Two funds that lost the deal cited the memo as why they'd wanted in.

## Aftermath, honestly

The fund's partner and Ledgerline's CEO had a genuinely productive board relationship (I checked in quarterly through my remaining two years at Marbury Cross — banking follow-through is a habit and also a lead source). The 2022 repricing cycle hit the category; Ledgerline's round was priced at the top but structured clean enough (weighted-average anti-dilution, no ratchet, sane pay-to-play) that the repricing was survivable rather than fatal. The company was acquired in 2024 at a price below the Series D post-money but above every preference stack's trigger — the structure fight in 2020 is a large part of why the outcome was a soft landing for common holders instead of a wipeout. Term sheets are destiny; the fight was worth every session.
