# Factor Xa reversal: a formulary review for the P&T committee

Hollisbrook Health, system pharmacy and therapeutics (P&T) committee. January to June 2023, with a re-review in September 2024. I led the review and wrote the monograph and the drug-use evaluation. On my resume it's one bullet. This file holds the evidence question, the options, the people involved, the version the committee sent back, and how each number is defined. All counts are aggregates from the drug-use evaluation; there are no patient details here.

## The question

Patients on apixaban or rivaroxaban who have a major bleed may need the anticoagulant reversed. Our formulary had two options: the targeted reversal agent for factor Xa inhibitors, unrestricted since 2019, and four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC). In 2022 the system spent $1.18M on the targeted agent across 58 treatment courses. The P&T chair asked pharmacy for a review: which agent, for which patients, under what rules.

The evidence was the hard part. The targeted agent's US approval rested on its effect on anti-factor Xa activity, and its evidence in bleeding patients came from a single-arm study. There was no randomized comparison with 4F-PCC at the time of our review. The observational comparisons pointed in different directions and carried obvious confounding, because which patient got which drug wasn't random. Using 4F-PCC for this purpose is off-label, though common. Published guidance differed: some documents listed the targeted agent first, others treated the two as alternatives.

## My role

I owned the review: the literature appraisal, the drug-use evaluation of every 2022 course, the cost model, the draft use criteria, and the presentation to the committee. I ran a workgroup with the neurocritical care pharmacist, an emergency medicine pharmacist, and physicians from emergency medicine, neurosurgery, neurocritical care and hematology. The committee, 22 voting members, made the decision. Pharmacy informatics built the order set.

I also asked both manufacturers for information through their medical-information lines and met with an MSL from each. One answered the questions I had sent, including data that didn't favor the product, and sent the full formulary dossier within a week. The other brought a slide deck that answered questions I hadn't asked. That pair of meetings is part of why I want this job, and I know which of the two I want to be.

## The options

1. Leave it unrestricted and educate prescribers. The drug-use evaluation showed 41% of 2022 courses outside the draft criteria. The workgroup agreed education alone wouldn't move that.
2. Remove the targeted agent and use 4F-PCC for all factor Xa reversal. Hematology was open to it on cost. The neurosurgery and neurocritical care physicians weren't, for bleeding in the head. The evidence couldn't settle it in either direction, and the committee wasn't going to remove an option for the highest-risk bleeds on thin evidence.
3. Restrict it, with an attending in neurosurgery or neurocritical care approving each use. My first draft.
4. Restrict it by written criteria that the pharmacist verifies at once, with the on-call attending only for orders outside them. Approved in June.

## How it went

- January and February: literature appraisal and the drug-use evaluation. Against the draft criteria, 24 of the 58 courses (41%) wouldn't have qualified. Most were bleeds outside the head where 4F-PCC was a reasonable choice, or the last anticoagulant dose was long enough ago that reversal wasn't needed.
- April: first committee meeting, with option 3. Emergency physicians objected that a phone approval adds minutes to a time-critical decision. They were right, and the committee sent the draft back.
- May: redesign to option 4. If an order meets the criteria (intracranial hemorrhage or another life-threatening bleed, and a factor Xa inhibitor taken within 18 hours or an anti-Xa level above the lab's threshold), the pharmacist verifies it at once. Only orders outside the criteria go to the on-call attending. 4F-PCC is the default for other major bleeds, with weight-based dosing in the same order set.
- June: approved, with 1 vote against, and reviews at 6 and 12 months.

## The numbers, and their definitions

- Drug spend, targeted agent: $1.18M in calendar 2022 (58 courses), $430K from July 2023 to June 2024 (21 courses). 4F-PCC spend for this use rose about $100K. Net change: about $650K a year lower.
- Criteria adherence: 20 of 21 courses met the criteria. The 1 exception went to the on-call attending, who approved it.
- Time to treatment: median minutes from order to administration, either agent, 44 before and 42 after. This was the balancing measure the emergency physicians asked for, and the one I watched most closely.
- Clinical outcomes: too few cases to compare. I said that in committee rather than show a trend I couldn't defend.

One number didn't match my model. If 34 of the 58 courses qualified, I expected about 34 a year under the criteria. There were 21. Part of the gap is that physicians chose 4F-PCC for some patients who did qualify, which the criteria allow, and which is where the extra 4F-PCC spend came from. So part of the saving came from a change in practice, not from the restriction itself. I told the committee that at the 12-month review.

## The re-review, September 2024

A randomized trial in intracranial hemorrhage, published in 2024, reported better control of bleeding with the targeted agent than with usual care, and more thrombotic events. I appraised it for the committee and put both effects side by side. The committee kept the criteria, which already reserve the agent for the highest-risk bleeds, and asked for a yearly review.

## What went wrong

- My first drug-use count was 44 courses, not 58. Doses given in the emergency department before admission were charted in a different part of the record, and my query missed them. A neurosurgeon on the workgroup noticed the gap before the committee did. I re-ran the evaluation and sent the correction in writing.
- The approval rule. I designed for cost control and didn't sit with the emergency physicians first. It cost two months.
- I met neurosurgery late. They cared most about bleeding in the head, and they first saw the criteria as a draft rather than from me in person.

## What I'd do differently

- Meet every specialty that uses the drug before drafting criteria, starting with the one that uses it under the most time pressure.
- Check any data query against a hand-pulled chart sample before reporting a count.
- Name the balancing measure in the first draft, not after someone asks for it.

This is the closest thing in my record to the MSL job seen from the other side of the table: I've been the formulary decision-maker asking a manufacturer's medical team for data, and I know what a useful answer looks like.

## What's still open

Whether the restriction changed outcomes for patients is still open. With 21 courses in the first year under the criteria, there are too few cases to compare, and I haven't claimed an effect either way. The criteria stay open to new evidence too: after the 2024 trial, the committee asked for a yearly review.
