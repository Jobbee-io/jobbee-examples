# Heart failure discharge study: a poster and a brief report

Hollisbrook University Hospital, cardiology teaching services. The checklist started in January 2022; the study covered July 2021 to June 2022; the poster was at the Northeast Cardiovascular Pharmacotherapy Symposium in October 2023; the brief report was published in a peer-reviewed hospital pharmacy journal in November 2024. I designed the checklist and the study and was senior author on both. On my resume this is one bullet under experience and two lines under publications. This file holds the design, the definitions, the rejection, and the confounder I almost buried. It's my one peer-reviewed publication, and I'd rather an interviewer hear its weaknesses from me. All results are aggregates. The project was reviewed as quality improvement, and no patient-level data left the hospital.

## What it was, and why

Patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) benefit from four drug classes together: a beta-blocker, an ARNI or ACE inhibitor or ARB, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, and an SGLT2 inhibitor. On our services, most patients left the hospital on two or three. Usually nobody had decided against the missing class. It just hadn't come up during a busy admission.

In January 2022 I started a checklist that the cardiology pharmacist runs on hospital day 2 and again the day before discharge. For each of the four classes: is the patient on it, and if not, is there a documented reason (low blood pressure, kidney function, potassium, cost, or another named reason)? The pharmacist raises any gap on rounds. The decision stays with the medical team.

## My role

I wrote the checklist and ran it on my services. I designed the study: a before/after comparison of HFrEF discharges from the two cardiology teaching services, July to December 2021 against January to June 2022. I wrote the quality-improvement request to the IRB office (they agreed it wasn't human subjects research), the data-abstraction manual and the analysis plan, and I abstracted about 40% of the charts myself.

A PGY1 resident from the 2022–23 class abstracted the rest and was first author on the poster. She had graduated by October 2023, so I presented it. The heart failure program director and a cardiology fellow were co-authors and checked the clinical definitions. A statistician from the research office ran the analysis.

## The numbers, and their definitions

- Population: 412 HFrEF discharges (ejection fraction 40% or lower), 198 before and 214 after. Patients discharged to hospice and patients who died in the hospital were excluded.
- Primary outcome: discharged on all four classes, or with a documented reason for each missing class. 19% before, 41% after.
- Sensitivity analysis, added after the first rejection: the same outcome counting only the three older classes. 34% before, 52% after.
- 30-day all-cause readmission: 22% before, 20% after. Not statistically different, and the study wasn't sized to detect a difference. I don't claim one.
- Safety: high potassium and kidney injury within 30 days. No meaningful difference, with small numbers.
- Pharmacist time: about 6 minutes per patient for each checklist run, timed over 2 weeks. That's the cost the cardiology division weighed when it kept the checklist after the study ended.

The checklist is still running. It's part of the cardiology pharmacist's daily work on both teaching services, and the residents who rotate with me run it too.

## What went wrong

- Definition drift. After 60 charts we saw that "on all four classes" alone counted good decisions not to start a drug as failures. We added "or a documented reason" and re-abstracted those 60 charts. The change was right, but it came after we'd started, and a reviewer caught it.
- The rejection. The first journal rejected the manuscript in February 2024. One reviewer named the definition change; the other named the confounder below. Both were fair.
- The confounder I tried to footnote. The 2022 US heart failure guideline, which made SGLT2 inhibitors a core class, came out in the middle of our after period. Some of the change was the field moving, not our checklist. On the poster it was one line under limitations. The three-class sensitivity analysis is the honest answer: the improvement holds without SGLT2 inhibitors, though it's smaller.

## Presenting it

At the poster session, an attending from another hospital asked within a minute whether the guideline explained the whole effect. I didn't have the sensitivity analysis yet. I told him the guideline timing was a real problem, that I couldn't separate the two effects with what was on the poster, and that I'd send him the analysis once we ran it. I sent it in December. In 2025 he was one of the speakers I invited to the same symposium. It's the kind of exchange an MSL has with investigators: a design, defined outcomes, a real limitation, and a straight answer when a physician challenges the data in public.

## What I'd do differently

- Write the analysis plan and pilot the abstraction manual on 20 charts before the first real chart.
- Plan for outside events. Guideline updates are predictable, and a concurrent comparison group would have handled this one better than a before/after design.
- Submit with the sensitivity analysis in the first version, not because a reviewer asked for it.

## What's still open

Whether the checklist lowers readmissions is still open. This study wasn't sized to show it, and I haven't run one that is.
