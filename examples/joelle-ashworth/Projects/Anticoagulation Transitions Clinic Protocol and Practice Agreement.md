# Anticoagulation Transitions Clinic: protocol and collaborative practice agreement

Companion to `Anticoagulation Transitions Clinic.md`, the overview.

I wrote the protocol and the agreement. They're described here in general clinical terms, with no patient details and nothing copied from a patient record.

## The full story

When I proposed the clinic in September 2021, the hard question wasn't what happens at a visit. It was what the pharmacist could change on their own. In my residency project at Larkhaven, 17% of DOAC discharges had a dose that didn't match the label. A clinic that could only recommend a fix would send each one back to a busy prescriber. So I wrote 2 documents: a protocol for the visit, and a collaborative practice agreement for what the pharmacist may change.

I drew the line at the label. Under the agreement the pharmacist may bring a DOAC dose to the label dose, order labs, write the plan for a procedure, and renew a prescription for up to 30 days. Anything that needs a physician's judgment goes back to the prescriber the same day. The cardiology division chief and the hospitalist section chief signed it. The medical staff office reviewed it, and the P&T committee approved it before the first visit in February 2022.

Two parts of version 1 didn't work. The booking was my design, built around what was easy for us, not for the patient. Patients were called after discharge to schedule, and from February to September 2022, 31% of booked visits were no-shows. Since October 2022 the visit is booked before the patient leaves, and video is offered. The visit note was a full consult note. In 2023 two cardiologists told me it was too long to use, and it was replaced by a one-screen handoff template.

The first renewal, in spring 2024, added patients switched from one anticoagulant to another. The physicians asked for it, because a switch carries the same dosing risk as a new start. It's part of why referrals passed 30 a month in 2024 and a second pharmacist took a half-day.

The line still holds. The 12% of out-of-label doses that aren't corrected at the visit are the cases the agreement keeps for a physician. Two changes are open for the next renewal: reaching the system's other 2 hospitals by video, and a standard second-visit check for patients whose dose changed. Before the first one, I'd want the concurrent comparison run, because extending the clinic would remove the only concurrent comparison group the system has.

## Who is referred

Included:

- Adults started on an oral anticoagulant during a Hollisbrook admission, for atrial fibrillation or flutter, or for a venous clot (a deep vein thrombosis or a pulmonary embolism).
- Discharged home from the cardiology teaching services, the heart failure service or the hospitalist services. Those are the services whose chiefs signed the agreement.
- Both DOACs and warfarin. A warfarin patient gets one transition visit and then goes to the hospital's warfarin clinic, which manages INR testing and dose changes long term.

Not included, and why:

- Discharge to a skilled nursing facility, rehabilitation or hospice. The facility manages the drug.
- Mechanical heart valves. These patients need warfarin, and the warfarin clinic takes them from the first day.
- Patients already followed by the warfarin clinic or by a cardiology practice's own anticoagulation nurse. A second follow-up would only confuse who's in charge.
- Patients restarted on an anticoagulant they were already taking at home. The risk the clinic targets is the new start.

## Booking

The referral is an order in the discharge order set. Since October 2022 the nurse navigator books the visit before the patient leaves the hospital, and the date and time go on the discharge paperwork. Patients choose in person or video.

The target is a visit within 10 days of discharge. The first visit is booked for 45 minutes. A 20-minute second visit is booked only when something changed at the first one or a question was left open, such as a lab result still pending.

## The visit

What the pharmacist covers, in this order:

1. Medication reconciliation against the discharge list: what the patient actually has at home, including over-the-counter drugs and supplements.
2. A dose check against the drug's label for this patient: the indication, kidney function measured the way that label specifies, age, body weight, and interacting drugs. Each DOAC has its own label criteria, so the protocol has a one-page table per drug.
3. Labs. If there's no kidney function test and blood count from the last 7 days, the pharmacist orders them.
4. Access. Does the patient have the drug, and can they keep paying for it? Cost and insurance approval problems are a common reason patients stop early. The pharmacist works them out with the outpatient pharmacy and the insurer.
5. How and when the patient takes it, and what to do after a missed dose, following the label for that drug.
6. Warning signs of bleeding or a clot, and who to call for each.
7. Upcoming procedures, such as dental work, endoscopy or surgery. The plan for holding the anticoagulant follows the hospital's procedure guideline, which depends on the drug, kidney function and the procedure's bleeding risk. The pharmacist writes the plan and confirms it with the clinician doing the procedure.
8. The handoff to whoever manages the drug long term.

Counseling is in plain language, with teach-back: the patient explains the plan in their own words before the visit ends.

## What the agreement allows

The collaborative practice agreement is a written agreement between the named clinic pharmacists and the physicians who signed it. Pennsylvania allows pharmacists to manage drug therapy under an agreement of this kind, within limits the agreement has to spell out. Ours was reviewed by the medical staff office and approved by the P&T committee before the first visit.

Under it, the clinic pharmacist may:

- change a DOAC dose to the label dose for the patient's current kidney function, age, weight and interacting drugs;
- order and review kidney function, blood counts and liver tests;
- write the plan for holding the drug around a procedure, following the hospital guideline;
- renew the anticoagulant prescription for up to 30 days, so nobody runs out while waiting for follow-up.

The pharmacist may not do the following, and sends each case to the prescriber the same day:

- change to a different anticoagulant;
- stop the anticoagulant;
- choose a dose for a patient the label doesn't address, such as a body weight or kidney function outside the range the label covers;
- change a dose the prescriber chose on purpose and documented, even if it's outside the label;
- change an antiplatelet drug taken alongside the anticoagulant.

That list is why 12% of the out-of-label doses found at first visits weren't corrected under the protocol. Each was one of these cases, and each needed a physician's judgment.

Escalation: signs of an active bleed or a new clot at the visit mean the patient goes to the emergency department the same day, and the pharmacist calls ahead.

## Who may work under it

Pharmacists named in the agreement who hold board certification or have completed a PGY1 residency, and who pass a competency check: 5 visits observed by me and a case-based test. Residents see patients during the ambulatory rotation, and I co-sign every change they make. The agreement is reviewed every 2 years. Each review re-checks the dose tables against the current labels and the hospital's guidelines.

## The handoff

The first version of the visit note was a full consult note. In 2023 two cardiologists told me it was too long to use. The handoff template that replaced it fits on one screen: the drug and dose, why that dose, what changed at the visit, the next lab due and who orders it, any procedure plan, and any open question. It goes to the receiving clinician the same day.

## How it has changed

- February 2022: version 1.
- October 2022: booking before discharge; video visits.
- 2023: the handoff template.
- Spring 2024, first renewal: the physicians asked to add patients switched from one anticoagulant to another during the admission, because a switch carries the same dosing risk as a new start. That change came after the 2-year outcome window, so it doesn't affect the comparison, and it's part of why referrals passed 30 a month in 2024.
- 2024: a second pharmacist's half-day.

## What it doesn't cover

- Long-term management. The clinic is one visit, sometimes two. Ongoing care belongs to the warfarin clinic or the patient's own doctors.
- Patients discharged from the system's other 2 hospitals. The agreement covers Hollisbrook's services only.
- Anything outside oral anticoagulation, even when the pharmacist notices it. Other drug problems go into the handoff for the receiving clinician.

## What I'd change in the next renewal

- Extend referral to the system's other 2 hospitals, by video, if their physicians sign on. That would also remove the only concurrent comparison group the system has (see the outcomes file), so I'd want that comparison run first.
- Add a standard check at the second visit for patients whose dose was changed, instead of leaving its content to the pharmacist.
