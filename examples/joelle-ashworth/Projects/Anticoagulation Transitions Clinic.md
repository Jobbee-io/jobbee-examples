# Anticoagulation Transitions Clinic

Hollisbrook University Hospital, Philadelphia. Proposed September 2021, first visit February 2022, still running. I built it and I run it. On my resume it's one bullet, but it's the project I'd expect an interviewer to spend the most time on, so it has 2 companion files. Companion files: `Anticoagulation Transitions Clinic Protocol and Practice Agreement.md` (who is referred, what happens at a visit, and what the pharmacist may and may not do under the agreement) and `Anticoagulation Transitions Clinic Outcomes Measurement.md` (how each outcome is defined and counted, the comparison and its weaknesses, and how the budget case was built). Every number is an aggregate from the clinic's quality reports. There are no patient details in any of the 3 files.

## What it is, and why

Patients who start a blood thinner in the hospital go home with a new high-risk drug and, often, a first follow-up weeks away. In my residency project at Larkhaven I found that 17% of patients discharged on a DOAC had a dose that didn't match the label for their kidney function, age, weight or interacting drugs. At Hollisbrook I saw the same pattern on our cardiology services, and nobody owned the gap between discharge and the first outpatient visit.

The clinic fills that gap. Patients newly started on an oral anticoagulant for atrial fibrillation or a venous clot during a Hollisbrook admission get a pharmacist visit, in person or by video, within 10 days of discharge. The visit covers dose, kidney function, interactions, adherence, bleeding warning signs, and the plan for upcoming procedures. Then we hand off to the patient's primary care doctor, cardiologist, or the hospital's long-standing warfarin clinic.

## My role

I wrote the proposal, the protocol, and the collaborative practice agreement, which the cardiology division chief and the hospitalist section chief co-signed. The agreement lets the clinic pharmacist adjust a DOAC dose and order labs under protocol; a change of drug goes back to the prescriber. I defined the outcomes with the quality department before launch, with one fix in month 2, and I report them every quarter. I staff the clinic 2 half-days a week. A second pharmacist covers 1 half-day, and a PGY1 resident joins during the ambulatory rotation.

Other people's parts: a nurse navigator, shared with another clinic, books the visits. Epic analysts built the referral order and the visit note. The quality department pulls the outcome data and runs the chart review against my definitions. The cardiology division chief defended the clinic at the budget meeting in 2023.

## Timeline and turning points

- September 2021: proposal, built on the residency finding and our own discharges.
- Late 2021: protocol and agreement approved; outcome definitions agreed with the quality department.
- February 2022: first visit.
- Month 2: the bleeding-severity threshold added to the outcome definition, and the baseline re-run.
- February to September 2022: 31% of scheduled patients didn't show. We called to schedule after discharge, and many patients didn't answer an unknown number.
- October 2022: the visit is booked before discharge, the date and time go on the discharge paperwork, and video is offered. No-shows fell to 12% and have stayed between 10% and 14%.
- Spring 2023: the clinic goes on the list for budget cuts. I rebuild the case; it stays open, with the cardiology division funding part of a pharmacist's time.
- 2023: a short handoff template, after two cardiologists told me our notes were too long to use.
- Spring 2024: the 2-year outcome comparison; the agreement's first renewal.
- 2024: the second pharmacist's half-day, when referrals passed 30 a month.
- October 2025: the outcomes and their limits, presented at the Northeast Cardiovascular Pharmacotherapy Symposium.

## The numbers that matter

- Volume, February 2022 to June 2026: 1,520 referrals and 1,170 patients seen (77%). The rest declined, were readmitted before the visit, or didn't come.
- Dose corrections: at the first visit, 13% of DOAC patients had a dose outside the label. I corrected 88% of those under the protocol and sent the rest to the prescriber, because they needed a judgment the protocol doesn't cover.
- Primary outcome: an emergency visit or readmission within 30 days of discharge for bleeding or a clot. Historical cohort, before the clinic (n=560): 6.2%. All referred patients, February 2022 to January 2024 (n=650): 4.4%. Patients actually seen in that window (n=505): 3.5%.

I lead with 4.4%, not 3.5%. Patients who come to a visit are healthier and more adherent to begin with, so the seen-only rate flatters the clinic. The comparison is before/after, not randomized, and the historical cohort includes the first COVID year. At these sample sizes the difference between 6.2% and 4.4% isn't statistically certain. I believe the clinic helps. I can't give you the exact size of the effect, and I say so when I present it. The outcomes file has the definitions, the confidence intervals and the weaknesses in full.

## What went wrong

- The no-show rate. I designed scheduling around what was easy for us, not for the patient, and those 8 months are on me.
- The budget case. My first estimate was $1.1M a year in avoided readmissions, counting every readmission the clinic plausibly touched. Finance didn't accept it, and the clinic went on the cut list. Rebuilt with finance's own method, counting only admissions with an anticoagulant-related primary diagnosis, it fell to $310K a year. The smaller number is the one they believed, and it kept the clinic open.
- I wrote the outcome definitions alone at first. In month 2 the quality analyst pointed out that "bleeding" needed a severity threshold, or every nosebleed would count.

## What I'd do differently

- Bring finance in at the proposal stage and agree how avoided readmissions are counted before the first patient.
- Book the visit before discharge from day 1.
- Set up the comparison with the quality team at the start, with a concurrent group if the hospital allowed it, instead of building a historical cohort afterward.

For an MSL role, the part I'd point to is the budget fight and the talk that followed: I've defended a number to people with every reason to doubt it, and I've learned to lead with the conservative one.
