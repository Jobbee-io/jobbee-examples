# Drug-information teaching for physicians and nurses

Hollisbrook University Hospital, September 2022 to now. On my resume this is a single bullet: "72 drug-information teaching sessions for physicians and nurses since September 2022." The two larger talks it led to are listed separately under presentations. It's the project closest to the scientific-communication part of an MSL's job.

## The full story

By 2022 I was answering the same drug questions on rounds week after week, to the few people standing there, and every new group of residents brought them back. In September 2022 the chief residents gave me a 20-minute monthly slot at the noon conference, and the hospitalist director let me repeat it at the hospitalists' meeting. I started nursing in-services on high-alert cardiac drugs at the same time, on the cardiac step-down unit and the cardiac ICU. The nurse educators there schedule them, and we wrote the nursing quiz together.

The start was rough. My first 4 physician sessions had 30-plus slides in 20 minutes, and residents left before the end. I cut each one to a single clinical question and 5 slides, and attendance held after that. The nursing sessions left out night shift for 8 months, until two night nurses pointed out that high-alert infusions run at 3 a.m. too. The recorded 12-minute versions came from that conversation.

From January 2023 I logged every drug question clinicians paged me with. The log now picks the topics: when a category grows, it becomes a session. Two PGY1 residents a year each present one session, after I go through it with them. The series led to Department of Medicine grand rounds in March 2025 and an invited talk at the Northeast Cardiovascular Pharmacotherapy Symposium in October 2025.

By September 2026 it was 72 sessions and about 1,500 people in the room. Across 410 paired nursing quizzes, the mean score went from 61% before a session to 87% after.

What's still open is whether any of it changes practice. On the one unit where we repeated the quiz 3 months later, the score had dropped to 74%. I tried to show fewer heparin dosing errors after a run of in-services, but the dosing-weight changes went live in the same period, and I can't separate the two. So I don't claim it. The series is still running.

## What it is

- Cardiology Drug Update: a 20-minute monthly session at the noon conference for cardiology fellows and medicine residents, repeated at the hospitalists' meeting. One clinical question, the evidence, and three things to do differently on Monday. 38 sessions through September 2026.
- Nursing in-services on high-alert cardiac drugs: heparin and bivalirudin infusions, amiodarone, milrinone and dobutamine, and the dosing-weight rules from `Dosing Weight Safety Change.md`. 34 sessions on the cardiac step-down unit and the cardiac ICU, plus recorded 12-minute versions for night shift since 2023.
- The larger talks: Department of Medicine grand rounds in March 2025 on anticoagulation reversal (about 180 attendees, accredited for continuing education through the hospital), and an invited talk at the Northeast Cardiovascular Pharmacotherapy Symposium in October 2025.

I choose the topics, write every session, present most of them, and run the nursing quiz. Two PGY1 residents a year each present one session, which I review with them first. The nurse educators on both units schedule the in-services and co-wrote the quiz. The chief residents and the hospitalist director gave me the time slots.

## The question log

Since January 2023 I've logged every drug question clinicians page me with: the asker's role (never a name), the topic, and how long the answer took. Through September 2026: 1,260 questions. Top categories: holding anticoagulants around procedures (22%), DOAC dosing in kidney disease (14%), switching between anticoagulants (11%), and heart failure drug titration (9%). When a category grows, it becomes a session. It's the closest thing I've done to gathering insights in the MSL sense: a structured record of what clinicians actually don't know, kept apart from what I assume they don't know.

## How a session is built

One clinical question, written the way the person asking would say it. Primary literature first, guidelines second, and a plain statement of what the evidence doesn't show. Teaching cases are composites I write from scratch; I never use a real patient's chart, even with details removed. 5 slides or fewer, and a one-page handout people keep in a pocket.

## The numbers, and their definitions

- Sessions: 72 (38 for physicians, 34 for nurses), September 2022 to September 2026.
- Attendance: about 1,500, from sign-in sheets. That counts people in the room, not learning.
- Nursing quiz: 5 questions before and after each in-service, 410 paired quizzes. Mean score 61% before, 87% after. On one unit we repeated it 3 months later: 74%.
- Recorded versions: 1,120 views in the hospital's learning system. I don't count a view as attendance.

## What went wrong

- My first 4 sessions were 30-plus slides in 20 minutes, and residents left before the end. I cut to one question and 5 slides, and attendance held after that.
- Night shift was left out for the first 8 months, until two night nurses pointed out that high-alert infusions run at 3 a.m. too. The recorded versions came from that conversation.
- I tried to show the teaching reduced errors by comparing heparin dosing-error reports on the step-down unit before and after a run of in-services. The dosing-weight changes went live in the same period, so I can't separate the two effects. I stopped claiming it.

## What changes in an MSL role

In the hospital I can teach any use the evidence supports, approved or not. As an MSL I'd discuss off-label data only in answer to an unsolicited question, and the content would have to be balanced and non-promotional. I know that rule from reading and from being on the receiving end of medical-affairs teams. I haven't worked under it yet, and I expect to learn it in practice, not only on paper.
