# SDR to enterprise AE — the path as a project

Corvidale Systems, 2013–2016, with the parts that kept running after I left. Most sales resumes treat the SDR era as a bad haircut you grow out of. Mine was three years of deliberate engineering — first of my own path, then of a program that ran without me. This file is the project record of both, because the SDR-to-enterprise path is a designed system or it's a lottery, and I've now seen it run both ways.

## The raw material

I joined Corvidale (sales analytics, ~120 people) in September 2013 as SDR #14, straight out of a BA in economics and a summer of door-to-door textbook sales — which is exactly as character-building and exactly as scalable as it sounds. SDR job: cold-call and email 60 touches a day into a mostly wrong ICP, hand "qualified" meetings to AEs, get graded on volume because volume was easy to measure.

Two things happened in year one that set the course:

- **I sourced a deal, not just a meeting.** Marrowfield Logistics — I found them on a hunch (a job post mentioning "pipeline spreadsheets"), researched their dispatch process for two weeks before first touch, and wrote the discovery questions the AE ended up using verbatim. Closed at $180K ARR eight months later. I got a spot bonus and, more importantly, proof that the *origin* of a deal is a skill, not a lottery ticket.
- **I watched three better-resourced SDRs wash out.** All three were working harder than me. What they lacked wasn't effort — it was a map of what the next seat actually required, and seniority of access to anyone who could tell them.

## First Call — the mentoring program (2014–2016)

I built **First Call** in 2014, quietly, as a peer thing: a biweekly hour where SDRs brought real calls (recorded, with permission) and one senior AE tore them apart live. Rules I'd keep today: no role-play, real calls only; the senior volunteer rotates monthly so nobody's style calcifies into gospel; and every session ends with one written takeaway posted to the team channel.

What it became by 2016:

- 6 SDRs in the founding pod → 18 SDRs across two offices in the program by my last quarter.
- Corvidale made it official (calendar block, AE-volunteer rotation, a one-page charter) after the first cohort produced two internal AE promotions — including mine.
- The charter's core document was the **"AE readiness map"**: the seven things an AE seat actually demands (discovery depth, multi-threading, forecast honesty, procurement literacy, product narrative, negotiation, and territory math), each with the observable behaviors that demonstrate it. SDRs self-assessed against it quarterly with their manager. It killed the "promotion is tenure" myth because the map was public and the gap was personal.
- The reason I trust the design: **First Call outlived me.** Corvidale ran it for at least two more cohorts after I left (I kept in touch with the pod leads), and the readiness map is the piece I've seen adapted since — one of the AEs I mentored shipped a version at her next company.

## Engineering my own path off the ladder (2015–2016)

- **Year 2: adjacent scope.** I took the sales-analytics side project nobody owned — pipeline hygiene reporting — because it put my name on the artifacts AEs and the VP read weekly. Influence with no authority, practice for a career of it.
- **The President's Club year (2015)**, as SDR team lead of a pod of six, on sourcing metrics — with the honest caveat that pod attainment was helped by a product launch that made every touch warmer. I'll take the club; I'll also name the tide.
- **The promotion case, built as a doc.** In Q4 2015 I wrote my own promotion case: the Meridian origin story, the First Call charter, the readiness map, and a 90-day plan for an AE seat that pre-answered "but she's never carried a number." My manager's only edit was the title of the doc. Promoted January 2016 into the mid-market seat at Bramblewood (I moved companies for the AE title — Corvidale's AE seats were frozen that year, and I wasn't going to wait out a hiring freeze at the rung I was climbing past).

## What the SDR era actually taught (and I still use)

- **Origin stories are a skill.** The Meridian pattern — research before touch, discovery questions written before the call — is the same muscle that later mapped a nine-person committee at Harvestlane in 90 days.
- **A promotion case is a product you ship to an internal customer.** Every promotion since — mid-market → enterprise, enterprise → principal — has been a written case with a 90-day plan. I've never been promoted by tenure, and I've never once been sorry I didn't wait for it.
- **Programs need a charter, not a champion.** First Call survived me because it had rules, a rotation, and a one-page doc — the same design principle that later kept my security pre-emption process alive across deals (`projects/procurement-security-win.md`).

## Honest ledger

- The textbook-sales summer taught me rejection tolerance and nothing else that transferred. I list it because it's the truth, not because it's a credential.
- I did not invent mentoring at Corvidale — two senior AEs (Tasha R. and Boyle) volunteered the first sessions before there was a program. First Call is a *container* I built around generosity that already existed, and I've tried to credit them by name in every retelling.
- My promotion to AE required leaving Corvidale. I'm proud of the path and I won't romanticize it: the company couldn't open the seat, the map didn't change that, and the move was the right call. A ladder you designed still has rungs other people control.
