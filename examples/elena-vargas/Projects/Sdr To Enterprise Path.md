# SDR to enterprise AE — the path as a project

Corvidale Systems, September 2013 to December 2015, plus the parts that kept running after I left. Two things in one file: the mentoring program I built for SDRs, and how I engineered my own move off the SDR rung.

## The starting point

I joined Corvidale (sales analytics, about 120 people) in September 2013 as the 14th SDR, straight out of an economics BA and a summer of door-to-door textbook sales. The job: 60 calls and emails a day into a mostly wrong customer profile, handing "qualified" meetings to AEs, graded on volume because volume was easy to measure.

Year one set the course:

- **I sourced a deal, not just a meeting.** Marrowfield Logistics: I found them through a job post that mentioned "pipeline spreadsheets," researched their dispatch process for two weeks before the first touch, and wrote the discovery questions the AE used word for word. It closed at $180K ARR eight months later. I got a spot bonus, and proof that the origin of a deal is a skill.
- **I watched three better-resourced SDRs wash out.** All three worked harder than I did. What they lacked was a map of what the next seat actually required, and access to anyone who could tell them.

## First Call, the mentoring program (2014–2015)

A biweekly hour where SDRs brought real recorded calls, with permission, and one senior AE took them apart live. The rules: no role-play, real calls only; the senior volunteer rotates monthly so nobody's style hardens into gospel; every session ends with one written takeaway posted to the team channel.

- It grew from the 6 SDRs in my founding pod to 18 SDRs across two offices by my last quarter.
- Corvidale made it official, with a calendar block, an AE volunteer rotation and a one-page charter, after the first cohort produced two internal AE promotions in 2015.
- The charter's core was the **AE readiness map**: the seven things an AE seat demands (discovery depth, multi-threading, forecast honesty, procurement literacy, product narrative, negotiation, territory math), each with the observable behaviors that show it. SDRs self-assessed against it every quarter with their manager. It killed the "promotion is tenure" myth, because the map was public and the gap was personal.
- It outlived me, because it had rules, a rotation and a one-page charter rather than depending on me. Corvidale ran at least two more cohorts after I left, and one AE I mentored shipped her own version of the readiness map at her next company.

## My own path off the rung

- **Adjacent scope.** In year two I took the pipeline-hygiene reporting nobody owned, because it put my name on the artifacts AEs and the VP read every week.
- **President's Club (SDR), 2015,** as team lead of a pod of six, on sourcing metrics. A product launch that year made every touch warmer. I'll take the club; I'll also name the tide.
- **The promotion case, written as a doc.** In Q4 2015 I wrote my own case: the Marrowfield origin story, the First Call charter, the readiness map, and a 90-day plan for an AE seat that pre-answered "but she's never carried a number." My manager signed off and changed only the title. Then Corvidale froze AE hiring for 2016. I took the same doc into my Bramblewood interviews and was hired in January 2016 into a mid-market AE seat. I wasn't going to wait out a hiring freeze on the rung I was climbing past.

## What carried forward

Every promotion since, mid-market to enterprise and enterprise to principal, has been a written case with a 90-day plan. And the Marrowfield habit (research before the first touch, discovery questions written before the call) is the one that named a nine-person committee at Harvestlane in 90 days.

## Honest ledger

- The textbook summer taught me rejection tolerance and nothing else that transferred.
- I didn't invent mentoring at Corvidale. Two senior AEs, Tasha R. and Boyle, volunteered the first sessions before there was a program. First Call is a container I built around generosity that already existed, and I credit them by name every time I tell this.
- My move to AE required leaving. A ladder you designed still has rungs other people control.
