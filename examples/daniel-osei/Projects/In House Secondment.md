# 6 months as in-house product counsel (secondment)

Bramhall Sayre LLP, seconded to a client's product counsel team, July–December 2022. The client was a private, late-stage consumer fintech company: a personal finance app with savings, a debit card with rewards, and payments, offered through a partner bank. 1,500–2,500 employees, a legal team of about 25, and a product counsel team of 5 reporting to an associate general counsel. On my resume this is one bullet. It gets a full file because it's the reason I'm applying for in-house jobs. It's also the honest limit of my in-house experience: 6 months, at one company. The client isn't named, and the reviews are described by type only.

## The full story

In July 2022, about 18 months after I started at the firm, I was seconded to a client's product counsel team for 6 months. Until then I'd advised on products from the outside. Now I sat with the team 4 days a week and covered 3 product teams: savings, card rewards and onboarding. The associate general counsel (AGC) reviewed anything high-risk. The product managers made the launch calls, and the privacy team made the privacy calls.

The first 2 months showed me how different the job is. In week 3 a product manager asked whether a referral promotion needed sweepstakes rules. I wrote a 6-page memo. She needed a yes or no by Friday, and the AGC told me to send her the answer and its one condition. In month 2 I held a card-rewards marketing page for 9 days over an "up to" claim. The marketing analytics team had the proof the whole time, and I hadn't asked who had it. The AGC settled it in a 15-minute call. That was the 1 launch I delayed, and the delay was mine. Also in month 2 I learned the partner bank's review calendar, which meant the dates I'd given for my first 2 launches were each a week wrong.

From month 3 I read product specs and sat in sprint demos, and I started catching issues in design reviews instead of the week before launch. I wrote a one-page checklist for marketing claims: what proof each type of claim needs, and who usually has it. By December I had done about 140 reviews, at a median of 2 business days against the team's 3-day target.

The secondment ended in December 2022 and I went back to the firm. There I changed how I write for clients: the answer and the risk on page 1. The team was still using the checklist in late 2023, the last time I asked.

What's still open is the size of the experience. It's 6 months at one company, and I haven't worked in-house since. What I know of consumer financial regulation is working knowledge, learned on 3 products with the AGC checking my work.

## The job

I sat with the team 4 days a week and covered 3 product teams: savings, card rewards and onboarding. I owned the legal review for those teams, with the associate general counsel (AGC) as my reviewer on anything high-risk. The product managers owned the launch decisions. The privacy team owned privacy decisions; I wrote up the issue and joined the meeting. I had no direct reports.

Anything touching the partner bank also went through the bank's compliance review, on the bank's calendar.

## The work, by type

About 140 reviews in 6 months, counted from the team's intake tracker:

- Launch reviews, about 55: new features, changes to existing ones, and the in-app copy that came with them.
- Marketing claims, about 45: savings rates, rewards, "no fees" language, and the disclosures each one needs.
- Promotions, about 15: referral bonuses and sweepstakes rules.
- Vendor contracts for the product teams, about 13.
- Privacy reviews with the privacy team, when a feature used data in a new way, about 10.
- User terms: 2 updates to the rewards terms.

The rules I worked under most were the federal rules on advertising deposit rates and on electronic payments and debit cards, and the general ban on unfair or deceptive practices. I learned them as they applied to 3 products, with the AGC checking my work. That's working knowledge of consumer financial regulation, not expertise, and I describe it that way.

A typical marketing call, by type: a "no fees" headline for the savings product, when the card linked to it could carry an out-of-network ATM fee. The fix was "no account fees" plus one disclosure line, not a rewrite of the page.

Median turnaround was 2 business days from intake to a written answer. The team's target was 3. 1 launch was delayed because of me, by 9 days (below).

## What went wrong

- Week 3. A product manager asked whether a referral promotion needed official sweepstakes rules. I wrote a 6-page memo. She needed a yes or no by Friday, with any conditions. The AGC read it and told me to send her the answer and the one condition, and keep the memo in case anyone asked. The answer was no: every user who qualified got the bonus, and without an element of chance it isn't a sweepstakes. The condition was that it could never turn into a drawing. Nobody ever asked for the memo.
- Month 2. I held a card-rewards marketing page for 9 days because I wanted proof for an "up to" rewards claim. The marketing analytics team had the proof the whole time; I hadn't asked who had it. The AGC settled it in a 15-minute call. The delay was mine, and avoidable. It's where I learned that the business owns the decision and I own how clear the risk is: how likely, how bad, and what it costs to fix later.
- I didn't learn the partner bank's review calendar until month 2, so the timelines I gave for my first 2 launches were each a week wrong.

## What I changed, there and after

- I read the product specs and sat in sprint demos. By month 3 I was catching issues in design reviews instead of the week before launch.
- I built a one-page checklist for marketing claims: what proof each type of claim needs, and who usually has it. The team was still using it in late 2023, the last time I asked.
- Back at the firm, I changed how I write for clients: the answer and the risk on page 1, the analysis after. Outside counsel memos get read as one paragraph.
- On any new team, I'd spend the first 2 weeks learning the calendar (outside reviews, the release schedule, who approves what) and ask "what do you need, and by when" before starting any piece of work.
