# Customer agreement playbook

Bramhall Sayre LLP, for a growth-stage B2B SaaS client (ARR in the $100M–$200M band, 2 in-house lawyers). Built March–July 2023, launched August 2023, version 2 in early 2024, handed over to the client in July 2024. On my resume this is one bullet with 2 numbers. It's my main commercial-counsel project, so this file holds the full version. The client isn't named. Positions are described by type, never by the client's actual figures.

## The starting point

The client sold to enterprises, and enterprises send their own paper or heavy redlines: about 300 negotiated agreements a year. Deals under $50K ACV mostly signed the standard order form without redlines, so everything here is about the deals above that line.

With 2 in-house lawyers, most enterprise redlines came to our firm. I'd been the lead associate on that work since early 2022, with a break for my secondment. Every redline went to a lawyer, including the ones where the answer was always the same.

The GC asked for 2 things: faster deals, and no surprises in what the company had already agreed to.

## The read that came first

Before writing anything, a 2nd-year associate and I read 180 signed agreements from 2021 and 2022. It took about 3 weeks. For each one we logged every clause the customer had changed and what the company had given.

- The company had given up more than it believed. 14 agreements had indemnities with no cap. 9 had service credits above anything in the standard terms. 6 gave the customer a right to terminate for convenience that finance didn't know about.
- About 80% of all customer edits fell on 11 clauses: limitation of liability, indemnities, data security, service levels and credits, termination, renewal and price increases, payment terms, assignment, insurance, governing law, and publicity.
- The same ask got different answers. The liability cap alone had been settled 7 different ways in 2022, depending on who negotiated it.

The read changed the brief. The GC had asked for speed first. After she saw the 14, the 9 and the 6, she put "no surprises" first. She decided to fix the uncapped indemnities at renewal rather than reopen signed deals, and finance re-forecast around the 6 termination rights.

## The options

1. A third in-house lawyer. The GC had budget for one more hire. The read argued against spending it on this: most redlines were the same 11 asks, and a third lawyer would mostly answer them a third way. She held the budget and used it in 2024 for a contracts manager to own the playbook once it existed.
2. A clause library only: approved wording, with no positions or approvers. About 4 weeks of work. Rejected, because the problem the read found wasn't wording. It was that nobody knew who could say yes to what.
3. A full playbook with approval tiers, run day to day by the deal desk. Chosen. About 5 months to build and train, next to my other matters.

The partner knew option 3 would cut the firm's fees on this work. He agreed because the client would send us more of its harder work instead, which is what happened.

## Who owned what

- I designed and wrote the playbook and the clause text, ran the training, defined the metrics, and ran the monthly escalation review for the first year.
- The GC owned every position. What the company will accept is a business-risk decision, and it was hers.
- The CFO signed off the walk-away positions on liability and service credits.
- The VP of Sales joined the workshop on termination, renewal and pricing terms.
- The deal-desk lead co-owned the escalation process and the tracking sheet.
- The 2nd-year associate did half the read and loaded the clause text into the client's contract tool.
- A partner reviewed the playbook before launch.

## How the playbook is built

24 clauses, each with the same 6 parts:

1. The standard position and its text.
2. Why the company holds it, in 2 or 3 plain sentences a seller can repeat to the customer. Sellers repeated them, and that was the point.
3. Fallback 1, which the deal desk can approve, with its text and any condition. Some fallbacks were available only above a deal-size band the CFO set.
4. Fallback 2, which in-house counsel approves.
5. The walk-away, which only the GC and CFO can change.
6. The defined terms to trace in the customer's paper before agreeing to anything that touches the clause. This rule came from my own 2021 miss (`Projects/The Carve Out I Missed.md`): every carve-out from the cap lists the definitions it depends on, and the reviewer checks each one in the customer's paper, not ours.

Pre-approved fallback text sat in the client's contract tool, so nobody retyped a clause from memory. The deal desk logged each negotiation in a tracking sheet: what the customer asked for, what we gave, and who approved it.

Customer paper got a separate page: where the 11 most-negotiated clauses usually sit in a customer's form, and a rule that any agreement on customer paper goes to a lawyer.

## Setting the positions

3 workshops with the GC in May and June 2023, 2 to 3 hours each. The CFO joined the second and the VP of Sales the third. My part was to bring the read's numbers (how often each ask came up, what the company had given before) and write each option with its cost. I didn't set positions. The arguments:

- Data-breach liability, the longest one. Sales wanted the option of uncapped liability for data breaches for the largest financial-services customers, who ask for it every time. The CFO refused uncapped liability in any deal. The GC set a separate, higher cap for data and security claims, with a fixed ceiling, as the standard; a higher ceiling as fallback 2; and uncapped as the walk-away.
- Service credits. Sales wanted the deal desk able to offer bigger credits. The CFO wanted credits capped as a share of monthly fees and kept as the only remedy for downtime. The settlement: credits stayed capped, and fallback 2 gave the customer a right to terminate after repeated service-level misses. The customer gets a real exit, and finance doesn't carry open-ended credits.
- Termination for convenience. After the 6 found in the read, the GC made it a walk-away unless the customer pays the fees for the rest of the current term.

## Training and launch

4 sessions for the 6-person deal desk in July 2023, built on anonymized redlines from the read. Then a 20-question redline test: 4 passed the first time, all 6 by the second try. Launch was in August. For the first month I reviewed every fallback-1 approval within a day of it being given.

## Timeline and turning points

- March 2023: kickoff and the 180-agreement read.
- April: read findings to the GC. The brief turns from speed first to no surprises first.
- May–June: 3 workshops; positions set.
- July: partner review, training, the redline test, clause text loaded into the contract tool.
- August: launch.
- September–October: the first 2 monthly reviews show fallback creep. Layout changed in October.
- Q4 2023: security reviews become the new bottleneck.
- Q1 2024: version 2, with a security-terms module.
- Spring 2024: the client hires a contracts manager.
- July 2024: the 12-month measurement. I hand the playbook to the contracts manager; the firm reviews it once a year.

## The numbers, and their definitions

Measured on negotiated agreements over $50K ACV: the 12 months before launch (284 agreements) against the 12 months after (312).

- Cycle time: business days from the first customer redline to both sides agreeing the final form. Signature routing isn't counted. Median 23 → 12.
- By paper, after launch: about 9 business days on the client's paper, about 21 on the customer's.
- Lawyer review: the share of agreements where an in-house or outside lawyer reviewed any redline. All of them → 38%. Customer-paper deals were about a quarter of the 312 and all went to a lawyer, so they make up most of the 38%.
- Fallback use: the share of deals where the deal desk opened with fallback 1. 41% in the first 2 months, 18% by month 5.
- Walk-away escalations: 9 in the first year. The company walked away from 4 of those deals: 3 over uncapped data-breach liability, 1 over a most-favored-customer pricing clause.
- Outside-counsel fees on customer agreements: down about 45% year over year. Those were my own firm's fees.

How the before number was built: there was no tracking sheet before launch, so I reconstructed the 284 deals from contract-tool timestamps and email. The after number comes from the tracking sheet. I checked 30 after-launch deals against the tool's timestamps, and 27 matched within a day. The before number is the weaker of the two, and I say so.

## What went wrong

- Fallback creep. Version 1 showed the standard position and fallback 1 side by side in one table. In the first 2 months the deal desk opened with fallback 1 in 41% of deals, which meant offering a higher liability cap before the customer had asked for one. I changed the layout: fallback 1 appears only after the customer has pushed back twice, and the deal desk logs what the customer asked for. Fallback use dropped to 18% by month 5. Sellers disliked the change.
- I left security terms out of version 1 to ship faster. Customers' security exhibits and questionnaires became the new bottleneck: about 30% of agreements in the first quarter still waited on a security review. Version 2 (Q1 2024) added a security-terms module, written with the client's security lead.
- The cycle-time definition flattered us. It starts at the first redline, so it misses the weeks some deals sit in the customer's procurement queue before any redline arrives. Sales leaders said the number looked better than deals felt. They were right. From Q2 2024 the tracking sheet also logs the day the contract was sent. Median from contract sent to agreed final form was 19 business days in Q2 and Q3 2024. There's no before number for it, so it can't show improvement; it shows what the customer experiences.

## Second-order effects

- The read's exposure list became the GC's renewal list. By the end of 2024, 8 of the 14 uncapped indemnities had been capped at renewal.
- The trace rule moved with me. I teach it in the first-year contracts bootcamp.
- The firm's work for the client shifted from routine redlines to the harder deals.
- The contracts manager role exists because the playbook does. A lawyer no longer has to own the day-to-day.

## What isn't solved

- Customer paper. It's a quarter of deals, every one still goes to a lawyer, and the median is about 21 days. The checklist helps a lawyer find the clauses faster; it doesn't let the deal desk handle customer paper alone. I proposed mapping the customer forms that come up most often to the playbook positions. As of my last annual review it hasn't been done.
- The security module ages fastest. Customers' questionnaires and the standards they cite change every year.

## What I'd defend, and what I'd do differently

- I'd defend reading 180 signed agreements before setting a single position. 3 weeks felt slow to the GC in March. It changed what she asked for.
- I'd defend hiding fallback 1 until the second pushback. It's the change that made the standard positions mean something.
- I'd put security terms in version 1, even if it shipped 6 weeks later.
- I'd design the layout with the deal desk in the room. I wrote it the way lawyers read, and the people using it weren't lawyers.
- I'd agree the metric definitions with sales, not only with legal, before launch.
