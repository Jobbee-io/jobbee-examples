# The carve-out I missed

Bramhall Sayre LLP, for a B2B data-analytics software client (revenue in the $50M–$100M band). I negotiated the agreement in October–November 2021, found my mistake in February 2023, and the fix was signed in June 2023. On my resume it's one line under failures. Neither the client nor its customer is named.

## The agreement

A 3-year customer agreement with a large financial-services customer, on the customer's paper, with an annual value in the $1M–$2M band. I was 11 months into practice. I held the pen, and a partner reviewed my issues list. The client's standard was a liability cap at 12 months of fees, with a separate, higher cap for data-security claims.

## What I agreed to, and why it was wrong

The customer asked for a mutual carve-out from the cap for "breach of confidentiality obligations." It's a common ask and it looked even-handed. I agreed and wrote it up as "mutual confidentiality carve-out, market."

The customer's paper defined "Confidential Information" to include "Customer Data." So any data breach on the client's side was also a breach of confidentiality, and outside the cap. The client's liability for a data breach under this contract had no limit, and the data-security cap it relied on did nothing.

The partner didn't catch it either. I don't count that as his miss: he reviewed my issues list, and the list described the clause by its name, not by what it did.

## How it was found and fixed

- February 2023, back from my secondment, I was reviewing another customer's paper for the same client. It had the same definition structure. I traced it, recognized it, and pulled the 2021 agreement that afternoon: same definition, same carve-out, signed.
- I told the supervising partner the same day. Within the week we told the client's GC in writing: what the clause did, what fixing it would take, and that it was my mistake. The firm didn't bill for any of the fix.
- Then I checked the client's other 31 customer agreements negotiated on customer paper since 2021. 1 more had a narrower version of the same problem. A side letter fixed it in the same quarter. The GC got 2 letters from us instead of 1, which is why I now check before I report.
- There had been no data incident under the contract, so no claim. The exposure lasted 19 months, from signing in November 2021 to the amendment in June 2023.
- The fix came in the renewal talks. The customer agreed to move data breaches under a separate cap of 3 times annual fees. To get it, the client gave up the price increase it had planned for the next 2 years. That was the real cost of my mistake, and the client paid it, not me. I don't publish the dollar figure: it's the client's number, and the band would identify the contract.

## What I changed

- I read the definitions before the carve-outs, and trace every carve-out through each defined term it uses, in the other side's paper, down to the last one. It takes about 10 minutes per agreement.
- My issues lists say what a clause does: "unlimited liability for any customer data breach," not "mutual confidentiality carve-out." The reviewer reviews the effect, not the label.
- When I find a mistake, I check every agreement with the same pattern before I report it, so the client hears it once.
- The trace became a written rule in the customer-agreement playbook I built later in 2023 (`Projects/Customer Agreement Playbook.md`). I teach this agreement, anonymized, in the first-year contracts bootcamp.

An in-house team will hand me hundreds of agreements nobody can read twice. How this one was found, reported and fixed is the most useful thing I can tell them about how I work.

## What's still open

Nothing is open. The June 2023 amendment ended the uncapped exposure, a side letter fixed the one other agreement with the same problem in 2023, and no claim arose while the exposure lasted.
